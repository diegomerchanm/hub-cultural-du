\begin{abstract}
Culturally-relevant activity in the Paris region is rarely centralized: it is spread across hundreds of
Instagram accounts and legible mainly to whoever already follows the
right ones. This thesis builds a pipeline that discovers accounts
through curated institutional seeds and a direct follower/following
crawl, ingests their profiles and posts into a Neo4j graph, and detects
events through a three-layer pipeline (semantic filtering, event-likelihood
classification, LLM-based extraction and gating) validated by three
rounds of blind human-versus-pipeline comparison, in which agreement
rose from 58\% to 96.7\% and precision from 56\% to 97.3\% on a random
sample — a targeted review of the twenty highest-scoring institutional
events instead found twelve of twenty problematic, the slice a
discovery product actually surfaces to visitors, and this thesis reports
that gap rather than the headline figure alone. Because an
automated account classifier selected a culturally valuable account
under 10\% of the time, hand curation of accounts replaces it as the
trust signal the corpus relies on, with every design decision behind
that pivot logged in real time so results remain traceable to the run
that produced them. The pipeline feeds a public, bilingual, static
discovery website with no backend, no user accounts and no personal
data collection, publishing through a staged human review, a rolling
retention window, and a deliberate manual deploy step reported here as
method. An earlier component, an exact computation of account
centrality (PageRank and betweenness), still contributes one term to
the site's ranking formula and one interface badge, but the account-selection
role it was built for has been superseded by human curation. The
thesis's contribution is methodological (curation as an evidenced, not
assumed, alternative to automated scoring; a blind evaluation protocol
that measurably drove three pipeline redesigns; a transparent account
of where automated signals fail silently), empirical (a categorised
network and a growing, increasingly accurate catalogue of 751 events at
the time of writing), and applied (a live, deployed tool usable by the
community it describes).
\end{abstract}

\section{Context and Motivation}
\label{sec:context}

Hub Cultural\footnote{Live discovery site:
\texttt{hub-cultural-du.diegomerchanm.workers.dev}. Source code for
the full pipeline described in this thesis:
\url{https://github.com/diegomerchanm/hub-cultural-du}.} began as an
attempt to make visible something that is well
known anecdotally but poorly documented empirically: the cultural life
that Latin American, and in particular Colombian, communities sustain in
the Paris region through small associations, independent venues, cultural
attachés, and a dense but scattered presence on Instagram. The author's
own Colombian background supplied both the initial motivation and the
first seed account for data collection — the Colombian consulate in
Paris (\texttt{@consuladocolparis}) — from which the network was grown
outward through automatic relationship discovery.

As the project progressed, the nationality-based boundary that had
organized it from the start — \emph{is this account or event part of the
Latin American diaspora?} — proved narrower than what the data actually
supported: many of the best-connected cultural accounts discovered
through the network turned out to be French institutions or
mixed-nationality venues with no diaspora affiliation at all, but
unmistakably part of the same cultural ecosystem the project set out to
map. The operative criterion is now cultural relevance rather than
nationality, with explicit priority — inherited from the project's
original four thematic agendas (\texttt{docs/objetivos.md}) — given to
photography, cinema, literature, theatre, and a fifth, less formally
bounded category: events organized by and for local communities,
regardless of origin. This thesis treats that shift as a methodological
finding rather than a footnote (\S\ref{sec:scope-evolution}): the
nationality criterion turned out to be encoded not only in the project's
stated scope but, measurably, in the event-detection pipeline's own
reference vocabulary — a finding with a direct, quantifiable effect on
what the system was able to see, detailed alongside the blind evaluation
that surfaced it (\S\ref{sec:eval-design}).

\section{Problem Statement and Research Question}
\label{sec:research-question}

The cultural activity produced by, and adjacent to, migrant and diaspora
communities in a host country is rarely centralized: it lives across
dozens of Instagram accounts, is announced inconsistently, and is legible
mainly to whoever already follows the right accounts. This is true of
Latin American cultural life in the Paris region specifically, but — as
this project's own data eventually made clear — it is not a problem
unique to any one national community; it is a structural feature of how
small-scale cultural production circulates on social media generally.

This thesis asks: \emph{How is culturally-relevant activity — with
priority given to photography, cinema, literature, theatre, and local
community cultural life — organized as a network of accounts and events
in the Paris region, and can that structure be systematically mapped,
analyzed, and made discoverable using publicly available Instagram
data?}

This breaks into three sub-questions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Structural.} Who are the connective actors in this
    network — which accounts function as hubs or bridges between
    otherwise separate cultural circles, and does this differ between
    institutional accounts and individual or grassroots ones?
    \item \textbf{Thematic.} How is cultural activity distributed across
    the project's priority domains (photography, cinema, literature,
    theatre, community events), and how well does an automated pipeline
    classify it into these domains compared to human judgment?
    \item \textbf{Practical.} Can the resulting graph be turned into a
    public-facing tool that helps the community it describes actually
    discover and attend these events — and what does building that tool
    reveal about the reliability of the underlying data?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Objectives}
\label{sec:objectives}

The project's objectives, as originally framed (\texttt{docs/objetivos.md})
and updated here to reflect the current, cultural-relevance-based scope:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Build and maintain four thematic monitoring agendas —
    \emph{literature}, \emph{photography}, \emph{cinema}, and
    \emph{theatre} — tracking the organizations that play an active role
    in each, complemented by a fifth, less formally bounded priority:
    cultural events organized by and for local communities.
    \item Consolidate a pipeline that extracts events from the accounts
    under these agendas, primarily from Instagram and, prospectively,
    from websites and other social networks.
    \item Map the network of actors within each thematic agenda and
    categorize them — by type of organization, geographic zone, cultural
    identity where relevant, and structural role in the network.
    \item Evaluate the participation opportunities these networks open
    up, using a deliberately human, non-technical success criterion: on
    this dimension, the project succeeds when it leads to actually
    meeting someone through it.
\end{enumerate}

Geographically the project centers on Île-de-France, though a meaningful
share of curated accounts and events originate from, or are organized by,
institutions physically located outside France (\S\ref{sec:limitations}).
Instagram is the sole data source, a deliberate boundary rather than an
oversight: it is the platform where this cultural activity is, in
practice, announced, and only public accounts are in scope. Inclusion is
governed by cultural relevance rather than national origin, with the four
thematic pillars and the community-events category acting as explicit
priorities rather than hard filters.

\chapter{Related Work and Positioning}
\label{ch:litreview}
% ============================================================================
This chapter positions the project briefly against the closest prior
work rather than surveying a full theoretical apparatus. A scoping pass
over comparable tools and a search of HAL and theses.fr found related
work on Instagram data, cultural events and diaspora communication
networks, but no project combining automated social-graph construction,
NLP-based event detection and human curation into a live discovery tool
for a migrant or cultural community in France.

The closest antecedents differ from this project on a specific point
each. Dana Diminescu's \emph{e-Diasporas Atlas}
\citep{diminescu2012,ediasporas} mapped migrant websites through
hyperlinks; this project substitutes the Instagram \texttt{@mention} for
that unit, a cheaper and more ephemeral signal. The Digital Methods
Initiative's \emph{4CAT} \citep{peeters2022,zeeschuimer} captures data
through manual browsing rather than automated scraping, trading scale for
closeness to observation. Sciences Po médialab's \emph{Hyphe}
\citep{jacomy2016hyphe} groups pages into researcher-defined entities,
where this project keeps one node per Instagram account with no merging.
\emph{Osintgraph} \citep{osintgraph} uses an almost identical technical
stack for individual surveillance rather than cultural mapping, which is
part of why this project's ethical boundaries are stated explicitly
(\S\ref{sec:ethics}). Declarative directories such as the IOM/UN
\emph{Diaspora Mapping Toolkit} \citep{iom2022diaspora} and
\emph{iDiaspora} \citep{idiaspora} list actors who register themselves;
this project is observational instead, surfacing accounts by detected
activity — each approach is blind to what the other sees.

\section{Research Design Overview}
\label{sec:research-design}

The project is organised as a seven-phase pipeline, each phase
implemented as one or more independent, re-runnable scripts writing to a
shared Neo4j graph, followed by the publication lifecycle described in
\S\ref{sec:lifecycle}. Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} lays out all ten
stages together with the script that implements each one. Script
filenames are prefixed by pipeline phase (\texttt{1\_harvest\_},
\texttt{2\_build\_}, \texttt{3\_analyze\_}, \texttt{4\_enrich\_},
\texttt{5\_export\_}, DD-019), so a script's place in the pipeline is
legible without opening it; the full source is public
(\S\ref{sec:context}).

Two design principles run through all seven phases and are treated in
this thesis as methodological rather than merely technical.
\emph{Idempotency}: every script can be re-run without duplicating or
corrupting what previous runs produced, because each checks a sentinel
property before processing (\texttt{eventExtracted}, \texttt{lat}) or
merges rather than inserts. \emph{Separation of provenance}: the manual
curation phase deliberately writes to a property namespace
(\texttt{artType}, \texttt{culturalIdentity}, \texttt{geoZone},
\texttt{manualFollowersCount}) disjoint from the one the ingestion phase
writes, so that neither can silently overwrite the other. The practical
consequence is that automated and human-produced data remain
distinguishable in the graph after the fact, which is what makes the
curation-as-trust-signal argument of \S\ref{sec:methodology-curation}
verifiable rather than merely asserted. There is no scheduler or
continuous integration: runs are manual and deliberate, since each
execution costs money (Apify credits, LLM tokens) and the point of a run
is usually to test a change rather than to keep a service fresh.
Ingestion writes entirely with \texttt{MERGE} rather than
\texttt{CREATE} and stamps every node with \texttt{firstSeenAt} on
creation and \texttt{lastUpdatedAt} on every write, giving a per-node
provenance trail without a separate audit table.

\section{Research Operations: Decision Logging, Run Tracking, and Iterative Safety}
\label{sec:research-ops}

Independently of the technical pipeline itself, the project maintained
three interlocking practices — a decision log, a run log, and
dry-run-first design — that make its evolution auditable, which matters
for an object of study that is a continuously growing graph, reprocessed
repeatedly by successive versions of the extraction logic.

\textbf{A real-time decision log.} Every non-trivial design choice was
recorded when made, in a sequentially numbered log
(\texttt{docs/decisions\_es.md}, running to DD-075 at the time of
writing, with some numbers reserved but never used).\footnote{The log
is maintained in Spanish, the language in which the project work was
conducted; \texttt{docs/decisions.md} holds a partial English
translation that currently stops at DD-033. Where entries are quoted in
this thesis, the translation is the author's.}
Each entry states the alternatives considered, the evidence behind the
final choice and, where applicable, validation results (the
deduplication resolver's own validation run is reported in full where it
belongs, \S\ref{sec:data-quality}, rather than repeated here). Recording
decisions in real time, rather than reconstructing them afterwards,
captures the alternatives considered and rejected \emph{at the time},
which retrospective documentation loses.

A second document, \texttt{CLAUDE.md}, serves a different purpose: read
by the AI coding assistant at the start of every session, it is
deliberately overwritten whenever it goes stale, describing the
pipeline's current state rather than its history. Together the two files
did most of the work of re-onboarding the assistant after each context
reset — current architecture from \texttt{CLAUDE.md}, reasoning behind
past choices from the decision log.

\textbf{A run log.} Every substantive execution of the pipeline against
live data was logged with its date, parameters and results
(\texttt{docs/runs\_log\_es.md}, RUN-001 to RUN-021), which made it
possible to distinguish a genuine change in the data from a change in
pipeline logic when comparing two exports, and to trace a decision back
to the run that motivated it.

\textbf{Dry-run-first design.} Every script writing to the live graph
ships with a \texttt{--dry-run} or \texttt{--diagnose} mode as a
first-class feature — \texttt{cleanup\_legacy\_accounts.py} runs its
deletion logic inside a transaction it rolls back under
\texttt{--dry-run}, so preview counts are exact rather than estimated —
which made it possible to iterate against a live, shared database
without risking an irreversible mistake. This did not hold
automatically, though: DD-038 records a production run that left almost
no auditable trace because its diagnostic export was gated on the same
flag; the fix restored parity between the two modes.

Together, these three practices make it possible to state, for any
result in Chapter~\ref{ch:results}, exactly which version of the
pipeline and which run produced it.

\section{Human-AI Collaboration in This Project}
\label{sec:ai-collaboration}

This project was built through a sustained collaboration between the
author and an AI coding assistant (Claude, Anthropic): the author
proposed a direction — a feature, a fix, a piece of analysis — usually
without first working out its technical feasibility, and the assistant
translated that into concrete, technically grounded options, stated
their trade-offs, and implemented the one selected, so that the author
interacted with the codebase almost exclusively at the level of intended
behaviour while file edits, refactors and commits remained the
assistant's responsibility throughout. The same division of labour
extended to the literature review, where an initial pool of roughly
forty candidate references was assembled by the assistant and then
verified and selected from by the author, rather than the two steps
running in parallel. At the other end of the range sits the blind
classification exercise behind the F1 figures of
\S\ref{sec:eval-design}, the one instrument that could not be delegated
at all, since judging whether a caption describes a genuine cultural
event requires a familiarity with the community the assistant does not
have. The pattern that holds regardless of how a given task was
organised: work that can be fully specified — code, drafted text,
summarised documentation — was substantially proposed by the assistant,
while situated judgement — whether a detected event is culturally real,
whether a candidate merge is correct — stayed with the author. The
decision log of \S\ref{sec:research-ops}, which records alternatives
considered rather than only the choice made, is what keeps the first
category auditable after the fact.

\section{Data Collection}
\label{sec:data-collection}

Data collection relies on Apify's hosted \texttt{instagram-profile-scraper}
and \texttt{instagram-post-scraper} actors rather than a self-maintained
browser-automation stack (DD-002): a hosted actor avoids the maintenance
and IP-blocking risk of scraping Instagram directly, at a metered
per-use price tracked in \texttt{.apify\_cost\_log.json} that constrains
sampling decisions. Profile and post scraping are separate scripts
(DD-003), since they use different actors, JSON structures and refresh
frequencies.

Seed selection grew from the single seed account described in
\S\ref{sec:context} (DD-006) into curated seed files
(\texttt{config/seeds\_*.json}) covering Latin American consulates and
embassies in France plus connected French cultural and academic
institutions (DD-022). Instagram's own \texttt{relatedProfiles}
suggestions add further candidates but are not treated as a trust
signal: they enter a human-reviewed candidate pool
(\texttt{discoveredViaCuratedAccount}, \S\ref{sec:methodology-curation})
rather than the corpus directly, and a later mechanism crawls the
follower and following lists of trusted institutional seeds directly.
DD-026 separates two questions the institutional seeds raise — whether
an account is a valid cultural-analysis target, and whether it is
structurally necessary to discover the network — assigning consulates
the role of seed source without treating them as cultural targets.

Post collection is recency-biased rather than density-maximising: the
harvester computes a per-account window from the gap since its last
known post, capped at a configurable maximum, merged into a rolling
window of the fifty most recent posts (DD-028, DD-029), since the
objective is detecting forthcoming events rather than reconstructing
publication history.

\section{Network Analysis: Status and Scope in This Thesis}
\label{sec:network-methods}
Graph-theoretic analysis of the account network — exact PageRank and
betweenness centrality, Leiden community detection, k-core decomposition,
participation coefficient and E-I index — runs offline with igraph and
leidenalg over two network layers, since Neo4j's Graph Data Science
library was unavailable in this environment (DD-013). An automated
classifier built on top of these metrics was originally the intended
criterion for deciding which accounts belonged in the corpus:
\S\ref{sec:methodology-curation} reports that under 10\% of the accounts
it selected turned out to be culturally valuable on manual review, so
hand curation replaced it as the operative trust signal. Community
detection, modularity and brokerage analysis are consequently not part
of this project's active methodology and are not reported as findings in
Chapter~\ref{ch:results}; the full design, together with its one
substantive result — some of the most structurally central accounts had
almost no content of their own, direct evidence against a content-based
selection criterion — is kept as closed work in
\S\ref{sec:future-work-network}.

One artefact of that earlier work remains live rather than archived: the
account-level PageRank and betweenness values it computed are still read
by the site's ranking formula (\S\ref{sec:ranking}, term $A$) and drive a
single interface badge marking structurally bridging accounts. That
residual dependency, and the caveat it carries — the values are not
recomputed on an ongoing basis — is documented where it actually matters,
alongside the formula itself, rather than here.

\section{Event Detection Pipeline}
\label{sec:event-pipeline}

Event detection runs in three layers over post captions.

\textbf{Layer 1 — semantic candidate filtering.} Each caption is embedded
with \texttt{paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM-L12-v2} and compared by cosine
similarity against a reference set of roughly 100 phrases covering
several event types, three languages and six announcement forms (calls,
openings, listings, date-plus-place, registration, reminders). Embeddings
rather than keywords were chosen because the corpus mixes Spanish, French
and English with emoji and slang, where keyword matching fails on
orthographic variation and indirect phrasing (DD-007); the reference set
is large rather than minimal (DD-010) because a narrow reference space
misses exactly the small community events the project most wants to
capture. Layer 1 exists primarily as a cost control: running the NLI
model over every post was measured at five to seven hours on the
available hardware, whereas pre-filtering reduces the candidate set to
roughly 40\% of the corpus and the total to about eighteen minutes
(DD-009).

\textbf{Layer 2 — is this event-like at all.} Surviving candidates are
passed to a binary natural-language-inference classifier
(\texttt{MoritzLaurer/multilingual-MiniLMv2-L6-mnli-xnli}). Two
decisions here are worth stating explicitly. The model is multilingual by
requirement, not by preference: an earlier English-trained NLI model
produced a detection rate of 0.7\% on Spanish and French captions —
effectively noise (DD-008). And the hypothesis sentence is now formulated
in the caption's own detected language, after a fixed-Spanish hypothesis
was shown to depress scores for English and French captions regardless of
their content.

\textbf{Layer 3 — LLM extraction, typing and gating.} Candidates that
pass Layer 2 are sent to a large language model — Ollama locally, or one
of four cloud providers tried in order with automatic failover and a cap
on rate-limit waiting (Groq, Google, Cerebras, DeepSeek, DD-033) — which
extracts title, description, date, location and price range in Spanish,
together with French translations of the title and description generated
in the same call at no extra request cost (\texttt{title\_fr},
\texttt{description\_fr}, DD-051, feeding the bilingual site of
\S\ref{sec:website}), assigns an event type from a fixed sixteen-label
taxonomy, proposes one to three free-form artistic tags (DD-042), and —
the decisive change — \emph{gates} node creation. If the model reports
that the post is not a public invitation, or not upcoming, no
\texttt{:Event} node is created at all; in the earlier design the node
was created regardless and merely scored lower, which
\S\ref{sec:eval-design} shows was the single largest source of false
positives. If a provider is exhausted or fails, the pipeline falls back
automatically across four cloud providers (Groq, Google, Cerebras,
DeepSeek, in that order — DD-050) rather than stalling the run.

Three further constraints shape the Layer 3 stage. Calls are deduplicated
by post identifier within a run, so co-published posts share one call.
Reasoning traces are requested only in dry-run mode, since they are
expensive and only useful for auditing. And a deterministic
\texttt{has\_text\_date} gate — requiring that a date actually appear in
the caption text — is evaluated \emph{before} the model is called rather
than after, which was measured to remove 40.7\% of calls offline and
48.3\% in production without changing a single final decision (DD-036).
The date extractor behind that gate is itself constrained to avoid
manufacturing precision: a month or year mentioned without a day is
discarded rather than resolved to an arbitrary first-of-month (DD-034),
after four disagreements in the evaluation sample were traced to exactly
that behaviour.

Newly created events inherit \texttt{artType}, \texttt{institutionType},
\texttt{culturalIdentity}, \texttt{geoZone} and \texttt{parentInstitution}
directly from the publishing account's manual curation, at no model cost,
whenever that account has been curated. Deduplication happens twice: an
inline resolver checks cosine similarity above 0.82 together with a
location match and a date within three days before creating a new node
rather than enriching an existing one, and a standalone resolver
(\S\ref{sec:event-resolver}) runs afterwards over the accumulated set.

\section{Event Deduplication}
\label{sec:event-resolver}

Several accounts routinely publish about the same event, so deduplication
is a structural feature of the corpus rather than an edge case. All pairs
of events are compared by embedding similarity first, over a vectorised
cosine matrix; dates function as mandatory confirmation rather than a
primary filter, so where both dates exist they must fall within three
days and similarity must exceed 0.75, and where one is missing a
stricter threshold of 0.85 compensates for the lost corroboration.
Location strings are not embedded, since text embeddings capture lexical
rather than geographic similarity — two different theatres sharing the
words "Théâtre de" would embed close together, while two addresses on the
same street might not. Location instead backs a lightweight guardrail
(DD-041): a merge is blocked only when both sides name a recognisable,
non-overlapping country, drawn from a small gazetteer built from observed
failure cases (generic community posts merging across cities that share
vocabulary but not geography, such as Paris with Madrid). The guardrail
is deliberately permissive — insufficient evidence on either side never
blocks a merge — and its residual limit is documented rather than
patched: it compares countries, not cities, so two different venues in
the same city can still merge, an accepted trade-off recorded in
RUN-021. The opposite failure — a missed merge — is also visible on the
live site: an exhibition or a recurring event announced more than once,
with shifting title or venue phrasing, can survive as separate cards,
since a merge depends on title-embedding similarity and a three-day
date window rather than on this guardrail, and neither is tuned for
content announced repeatedly over a long run.

\section{Deciding What to Trust: Curation and Blind Evaluation}
\label{sec:trust}

Two questions govern what reaches the graph: which accounts belong in
the corpus, and which of their posts are genuine events. The first is
answered by hand curation, the second by a blind comparison between the
pipeline's decisions and the author's own. They are reported together
because the second is measured against the criterion the first encodes.

\subsection{Human Curation as the Account-Level Trust Signal}
\label{sec:methodology-curation}

The project's original design selected accounts automatically: a
classifier scored geographic and cultural similarity against reference
embeddings plus hard profile signals, keeping accounts above a
threshold. In practice, well under 10\% of the accounts it selected
turned out to be culturally valuable on manual review, so the pipeline's
authoritative signal shifted from that score to
\texttt{manualDataCuratedAt} — written only by
\texttt{load\_manual\_account\_categorization.py} when an account
appears in the hand-curated spreadsheet.

Three mechanisms explain the gap. The score rewarded visibility over
cultural relevance, so well-documented commercial and institutional
accounts outscored sparse small collectives. Its geographic component
trusted a business-address field that was often wrong, patched first
with a bounding-box check on latitude and longitude (DD-031) and then
with a narrow exception for Alliance Française branches in Latin
American cities, whose bios mention France as a subject rather than a
location (DD-032). And structural importance is not content volume: the
closed network analysis of \S\ref{sec:future-work-network} found
top-bridging, top-PageRank accounts with almost no posts of their own,
mentioned into relevance entirely by others — any criterion reading
relevance off an account's own output misses these.

Curation is reconciled with discovery — every Instagram
\texttt{relatedProfiles} suggestion creates one account node on each
scrape — by tagging rather than deleting: an uncurated account reachable
via \texttt{RELATED\_TO} from a curated one is marked
\texttt{discoveredViaCuratedAccount} and kept as a review queue rather
than entering the corpus. Human curation does not scale
(\S\ref{sec:limitations}), so the corpus's boundary is set by how much
one researcher can review.

\section{Geospatial Enrichment}
\label{sec:geo}

Location strings extracted by the event pipeline are geocoded with
Nominatim, the OpenStreetMap geocoder, chosen because it is free,
requires no API key, covers both France and Colombia adequately, and
imposes a rate limit (one request per second, implemented here as 1.1
seconds between requests) that is tolerable at this corpus size (DD-018).
Resolved locations are placed in an administrative hierarchy —
\texttt{Location} $\to$ \texttt{Arrondissement} $\to$ \texttt{City}
$\to$ \texttt{Country} — with Paris arrondissements derived from postal
codes in the 750XX range. Sessions are logged so that a partial run can
be resumed rather than repeated.

Roughly one event in seven never acquires coordinates, for two distinct
reasons: some have no location text in the caption at all, and some have
text that Nominatim cannot resolve. A city-level fallback was rejected as
a display strategy because it produces coordinates that look precise and
are wrong — an event placed at the centre of Paris because its address
could not be parsed is indistinguishable, on a map, from an event that
really is there. The discovery site (\S\ref{sec:website}) therefore shows
only events with genuinely resolved coordinates; this is a presentation
filter, not a deletion, since the \texttt{:Event} node remains in the
graph for analysis.

\section{Ranking and Relevance Scoring}
\label{sec:ranking}

The discovery website ranks events with an explicit, documented formula
rather than an opaque model, on the stated principle of contextual
algorithmic curation rather than personalisation: there are no user
accounts, and the same conditions produce the same ordering for every
visitor. All component signals are converted to percentile ranks over the
universe of publishable events before being combined. The score is

\[
\text{Relevance} = 100 \cdot \left(0.30\,Q + 0.22\,A + 0.18\,B
+ 0.20\,T + 0.10\,C\right) \cdot P
\]

where $Q$ is detection quality ($0.70 \cdot \texttt{eventScore} + 0.30
\cdot \texttt{confidence}$); $A$ is the authority of the publishing
account in the network, itself a weighted blend of PageRank (0.40),
betweenness (0.25), follower count (0.20), participation coefficient
(0.10) and verified status (0.05); $B$ is social resonance (hotness score
and post count); $T$ is temporal proximity, implemented as explicit
steps rather than an exponential decay so that it can be read and
defended; and $C$ is session context. $P$ is a multiplicative penalty or
bonus: political accounts $\times 0.55$, low-confidence extractions
$\times 0.80$, free events $\times 1.05$.

Three aspects of this formula are methodological choices rather than
tuning. First, follower count is deliberately the smallest term in $A$:
a consulate with forty thousand followers is not thereby more culturally
relevant than a collective with three thousand, and PageRank is weighted
highest precisely because it measures recognition \emph{within the
cultural network} rather than general reach. Second, $Q$, $A$, $B$ and
$P$ are precomputed at export time because they require percentile
context over the whole dataset, whereas $T$ and $C$ are computed in the
browser on each visit because they depend on the current date and on the
individual session — precomputing them would freeze the ranking to the
last export (DD-043, DD-044). Third, and discussed at length in
\S\ref{sec:scope-evolution}, an earlier version of the formula included a
$\times 1.05$ bonus for any event carrying a non-null
\texttt{culturalIdentity} value; it was removed when the project's scope
broadened. A mandatory diversification pass then caps how many events
from the same author, category or parent institution can appear together
at the top of the home page, without which the largest category
mechanically dominates the front page.

A caveat belongs here rather than only in \S\ref{sec:network-methods}:
the PageRank and betweenness values behind $A$ are not recomputed on a
running basis. They are read off whichever account happened to be
covered by the last executed pass of the offline network analysis, so an
account curated afterward, or a shift in who mentions whom since that
pass, is not reflected in $A$ until the analysis is deliberately re-run.
This is a live product dependency on a component this thesis otherwise
treats as a closed, superseded line of work
(\S\ref{sec:network-methods}); it is reported honestly here rather
than smoothed over, precisely because it is the one place where that
earlier work still has a measurable effect on what a visitor sees.

\section{Publication Lifecycle and Event Retirement}
\label{sec:lifecycle}

Detection and ranking are only two stages of a longer cycle that this
thesis treats as methodology rather than as an implementation footnote,
because it is what makes the published catalogue trustworthy over time
rather than only accurate at the moment of extraction. Three mechanisms
close that loop, in the order an event actually passes through them.

\textbf{Staged human review.} Every \texttt{:Event} node created by the
extraction pipeline (\S\ref{sec:event-pipeline}) is tagged
\texttt{:PendingReview} at the moment it is created, and both the JSON
export that feeds the website and the Excel export used internally
exclude that label by default. Concretely, this means the Layer 3 gate
(\S\ref{sec:event-pipeline}) is a necessary but not sufficient condition
for publication: passing it only qualifies an event to enter a review
queue, not to appear on the site. A small local application
(\texttt{review\_events.py}) lets the researcher approve, edit or reject
each pending event; rejection is deliberately soft — it adds a
\texttt{:Rejected} label rather than deleting the node — so that no
review decision is irreversible (DD-047). This
reuses the same label-based status pattern already used for the
automated gate rather than introducing a second status system for human
decisions, and it is the direct methodological answer to
\S\ref{sec:data-quality}'s finding that twelve of twenty
highest-resonance institutional events were, on manual inspection, either
not events at all or misclassified: automated gating alone was not
sufficient to keep the published catalogue clean, and a second,
human-executed gate was added downstream of it rather than tuning the
first one further.

\textbf{A rolling retention window, not deletion.} Consistent with the
project's broader practice of never deleting research data outright
(rejection above, and the researcher-only tier of the Excel export),
\texttt{:Event} nodes are never removed from the graph once approved.
Instead, retention is enforced only at export time: the export query
excludes any event more than a fixed number of days in the past (thirty
by default, configurable per run), and the website's own client-side
logic enforces the same cutoff a second time as a deliberate redundancy,
so that a stale export cannot silently resurrect old events even if the
cutoff at generation time were ever bypassed. The researcher-facing Excel
export is exempt by design: it remains the full historical archive for
analysis, while the public site only ever shows what is current. Before
this cutoff existed, 71\% of parseable events in the graph were already
more than thirty days old and every one of them was still being shipped
to every visitor; the fix is a filter on read, not a change to what is
stored, which keeps the analytical record in Chapter~\ref{ch:results}
intact regardless of what the live site happens to be showing on a
given day.

\textbf{Publishing is a deliberate, separate step from generating data.}
Regenerating the exported JSON file, regenerating the static
search-engine-facing pages built from it, and actually deploying the
site to its hosting platform are three distinct manual commands, none of
which triggers another. This was not the original design intent so much
as a gap discovered in production: an approved batch of events did not
appear on the live site because the data had been re-exported but never
redeployed, and the site did not look broken, only stale, which is
precisely why the gap went unnoticed for a period (DD-053). Collapsing
the three steps into one command is listed as future work
(\S\ref{sec:future-work}).

\section{The Discovery Website}
\label{sec:website}

The public artifact is live at
\texttt{hub-cultural-du.diegomerchanm.workers.dev}. It is a static
site: \texttt{5\_export\_dashboard\_data.py}
reads the graph and writes a single JSON file, and \texttt{site/}
contains hand-written HTML, CSS and vanilla JavaScript that consumes it,
deployed to Cloudflare Workers as a static-assets bundle
(\texttt{site/wrangler.jsonc}, Workers Assets). There is no backend, no
framework and no build step, since the publishable events fit comfortably
in a file of a few hundred kilobytes and the site must be reconnectable
from a plain repository without configuration (DD-043, DD-044).

Four design decisions are worth reporting as part of the thesis's
applied contribution. The category menu is fully dynamic — assembled on
each load from the union of event categories and per-event artistic tags
actually present in the data, with real counts ordered by volume — so it
reflects what the upstream curation has prioritised rather than an
assertion in the code. Session context is stored in
\texttt{localStorage} rather than behind a login: category weights
increment when a visitor filters by or opens an event in that category,
decay daily, and are dropped below a floor, which yields the $C$
component of the ranking formula with no server and no personal data.
The export computes cosine similarity across all events and stores, per
event, the identifiers of its five nearest neighbours, while the
underlying 384-dimensional vectors are discarded and never reach the
client. And the first version deliberately has no dedicated
organiser-profile or methodology pages — those screens are more legible
as thesis material than as product, and are listed as future work
(\S\ref{sec:future-work}) alongside Explore and Map.

Event content is bilingual only going forward, by design: interface text
already switched between Spanish and French, but \texttt{title} and
\texttt{description} were written once, in Spanish, by Layer 3. French
versions are now generated by the same LLM call that extracts the event
(DD-051) rather than backfilled onto the existing corpus, and the
frontend falls back to the Spanish text whenever the French field is
absent rather than leaving a blank card.

Screenshots of the home screen, category filter and event detail panel
are not reproduced here — the live site
(\S\ref{sec:context}) is the more faithful record, and the interactions
described above are easier to verify by visiting it than by reading a
static capture.

\section{Ethical Considerations}
\label{sec:ethics}

The corpus is restricted to public accounts and publicly visible posts;
private accounts are labelled and not scraped. Organisational accounts —
venues, institutions, collectives — are treated as public actors and
named directly, since their posts are promotional by intent; individuals
appearing incidentally in captions, comments or tags are not the object
of analysis, and the cleanup phase removes accounts never touched by
manual curation together with their posts and comments, so this
periphery is not retained indefinitely. The public site stores no
personal data on any server — it is fully static, session preferences
live in the browser's \texttt{localStorage} with no third-party cookies,
and event cards link back to the original Instagram post rather than
reproducing it — and discloses that detection is automated, which
matters since a language model sits in the pipeline and, as
\S\ref{sec:limitations} documents, is wrong a measurable share of the
time.

\subsection{Legal Basis: GDPR and Instagram's Terms of Service}
\label{sec:gdpr}

Public accessibility does not remove personal data from GDPR's scope, so
this is addressed rather than assumed away. The basis relied on is
legitimate interest (Article 6(1)(f)): a genuine, non-commercial academic
purpose, necessary at this scale to answer a structural question about a
whole network, balanced by the data having been manifestly made public
by the accounts themselves and by the minimisation already in place
(individuals are not the object of analysis, the uncurated periphery is
deleted, \S\ref{sec:ethics}). One field is a residual risk rather than a
clean fit — \texttt{culturalIdentity} can encode an individual's ethnic
or national origin, and Article 9(2)(e)'s public-data exception holds
less cleanly where it reflects the curator's own reading rather than the
subject's own statement; it is optional, curator-observed, and never
feeds an automated decision (\S\ref{sec:limitations}). Individual
notification (Article 14) is exempted for research under
Article 14(5)(b) as disproportionate at this scale. Separately, scraping
via Apify rather than Instagram's own API contravenes Instagram's Terms
of Service — a known, accepted risk, not an oversight. This is a
non-lawyer's risk analysis, not a compliance certification.

\section{Limitations of Method}
\label{sec:limitations}

The limitations below are drawn from failures actually observed in this
project's runs, not from a generic list of caveats.

\textbf{Geocoding produces false precision when it fails.} Around one
event in seven has no usable coordinates, and the available fallback —
resolving to a city-level hint — yields plausible-looking but wrong
positions. The mitigation is exclusion from the map rather than
approximation (\S\ref{sec:geo}), which trades coverage for honesty.

\textbf{Date extraction is the pipeline's most fragile component.}
Successive rounds of evaluation found dates invented from a bare month
(DD-034), a theatre season "26/27" parsed as a calendar date, dates lost
because the extractor truncated captions at 600 characters (DD-037), and
implausible dates surviving a clamp that was silently skipped whenever
either of two date parses threw — producing events dated 2036, 2052 and
2090 from text that mentioned an anniversary, an age limit and a duration
in minutes (DD-039). Each was fixed once found; the pattern worth
reporting is that all of them failed silently, and all were found by
manual inspection rather than by any automated check.

\textbf{LLM classification is inconsistent at the margin.} RUN-020
recorded two nearly identical posts about the same event receiving
opposite verdicts, with no identifiable code cause. Layer 3 is also
capable of typing an event with a label meaning "not a cultural event"
while the surrounding logic created the node anyway, because creation did
not consult the category field (DD-039).

\textbf{Instagram-only sourcing bounds what can be observed.}
Organisations that announce primarily through mailing lists, their own
websites or other platforms are absent by construction. The primary
discovery mechanism (curated seed files and a direct follower/following
crawl, \S\ref{sec:data-collection}) does not depend on Instagram's own
recommendations, but the one secondary channel that does,
\texttt{relatedProfiles}, is shaped by the platform's own visibility
dynamics as to which further candidates it can surface at all — which is
exactly why that channel feeds a human-reviewed candidate pool rather
than the corpus directly. The algorithmic layer of the network is a
proprietary artefact and is analysed separately for that reason
(\S\ref{sec:network-methods}).

\textbf{Curation does not scale, and the tier labels are sparse.} The
trust signal that replaced automated scoring is one researcher's
judgement. The consequence is visible in the network analysis: at the
time of RUN-015 only 62 of 7{,}269 account nodes carried an explicit
\texttt{tier} label — the primary/secondary/excluded classification the
project's original, since-retired account classifier assigned — so
tier-based comparisons describe a small hand-curated subset rather than
the graph as a whole, and this must be stated wherever such comparisons
appear.

\textbf{Geographic scope leaks through automatic discovery.} Accounts
outside the intended geography enter the graph through
\texttt{relatedProfiles} before curation catches them — Alliance
Française branches in Colombian cities being the recurring case (DD-032)
— and at least one event in the institutional sample examined in
\S\ref{sec:data-quality} was a restaurant menu in Spain with no
connection to the project at all.

\textbf{A property inherited from the account is used as a proxy for the
event's own location.} Every \texttt{:Event} node inherits
\texttt{geoZone} from the account that published it, at no additional
extraction cost, and that inheritance has since become an operational
filter: the human-review interface groups pending events by it for bulk
triage (\S\ref{sec:lifecycle}, DD-052). A Paris-based account can announce
something happening elsewhere, however, so the property describes who
posted, not necessarily where the event is. That the first bulk-filtered
batch matched the reviewer's manual judgement in practice is evidence
the heuristic works for the common case, not evidence that it is correct
for every event it groups.

\section{Anatomy of a Published Event}
\label{sec:event-anatomy}

a different point in the process, and confusing this with a single
extraction step understates how much of the published record depends on
work other than the language model call. Table~\ref{tab:event-anatomy}
traces each group of fields to the stage that creates it; the full
property-level schema, including \texttt{:Account} fields, is reproduced
in Appendix~\ref{app:schema}.

The practical consequence is that two events with identical titles and
dates can differ in which of these fields they carry, purely because of
when each was created relative to a given pipeline change: an event
created before DD-051 has no French title regardless of how the site
currently behaves, and an event whose publishing account was curated
after the event itself was extracted inherits none of that account's
\texttt{artType} or \texttt{geoZone} until the extractor runs again over
new posts from that account. What looks, on the published site, like one
consistent object is therefore better understood as a record whose
completeness reflects the pipeline's own history as much as it reflects
the event itself.

\section{Geographic Distribution}
\label{sec:geographic}

Of the 559 events meeting the validity criteria on 2026-08-13, 528
(94.5\%) had an associated \texttt{:Location} node and 478 (85.5\%) had
real coordinates. The two shortfalls have different causes: 31 events
carry no location text at all, because the caption never stated one, and
50 have location text that either failed to resolve in Nominatim or had
not yet been through a geocoding pass. Only the first group is
irrecoverable without a change to the extraction prompt.

These figures do not reconcile cleanly with the 170 events that reached
the 2026-08-14 export (\S\ref{sec:thematic}) or the 71 confirmed
trustworthy the next day (\S\ref{sec:data-quality}) — not a bookkeeping
error, but three snapshots of the same geocoding backlog in motion: 478
is a same-day graph query mid-geocoding; 170 is an export snapshot taken
hours earlier, under a since-revised policy (DD-045, verified against
git history) that then dropped an event entirely rather than just its map
pin when geocoding looked unreliable; and 71 is that same batch after the
next day's promiscuous-coordinate diagnostic. Consistent with, not in
tension with, the intermittent-failure pattern discussed above.

Against the larger, current catalogue of 751 events, the shortfall is
wider rather than narrower: 91.7\% carry some location text (a venue name
or address, short of a full coordinate pair), but only 47.4\% have
resolved coordinates. The gap between those two figures — text present
but unresolved — is the geocoding backlog rather than a caption-level
absence, consistent with an intermittent Nominatim failure observed on a
subsequent full run (zero coordinates resolved out of 276 attempted,
cause unconfirmed) rather than with a change in how often captions state
a location at all. Because the discovery site excludes ungeocoded events
from anything geography-dependent rather than approximating their
position (\S\ref{sec:geo}), this backlog directly determines how much of
the catalogue is usable in a geography-first product, and geocoding
coverage is consequently one of the more consequential, and cheaper to
fix, current data-quality gaps.

A direct HTTP check against Nominatim, issued outside the pipeline with
the same compliant custom \texttt{user\_agent} the geocoder script sets,
returned \texttt{200} and a valid, well-formed response for a routine
query (\texttt{Paris, France}). This rules out a blanket API-level block
or a rejected user agent as the cause of the zero-coordinate run reported
above. The fault is therefore more likely intermittent or specific to
particular queries — rate-limiting under the pipeline's own request
volume, malformed location strings for some events, or a transient outage
during that specific run — rather than a persistent configuration error,
though which of these applies has not yet been isolated. The
arrondissement/commune breakdown that would substantiate the
Île-de-France scope claim of \S\ref{sec:objectives} with actual numbers
remains open until a geocoding pass with recovered coverage has been
completed and requeried.

\section{Data Quality Findings}
\label{sec:data-quality}
The failure modes catalogued in \S\ref{sec:limitations} are reported
here with the measurements attached to them.

A manual review of the twenty highest-scoring institutional events found
a much worse rate than the aggregate figures of \S\ref{sec:eval-design}
suggest: roughly eight were genuine public events correctly categorised,
seven were not events at all (interviews, podcasts, press releases, a
private meeting) that had passed the public-invitation gate, and five
were real events in the wrong category. Twelve of twenty problematic is
explained by how the sample was chosen: highest resonance within the
institutional category is exactly where the pipeline's two known
weaknesses concentrate — a leaky invitation gate, and a tendency to type
an event by the publishing account rather than by the post's own
content. Aggregate accuracy over a random sample and accuracy within a
targeted high-visibility slice are different quantities, and a discovery
product surfaces the second.

Deduplication performance is measurable on the same run. Over 664 events,
the resolver evaluated 220{,}116 pairs in roughly twenty seconds,
identified 68 duplicate pairs, and consolidated them through 189
relationship redirections, transitive chains included. Of the pairs it
rejected, 12{,}375 failed on similarity, 165{,}018 on the date window,
and 6 on the geographic-conflict guardrail — the last group being
precisely the failure cases that motivated the guardrail
(\S\ref{sec:event-resolver}).

A live run on 2026-08-15 (DD-045) puts a number on the geocoding false
precision: of 170 events that then carried some coordinate, 74 shared
one of four "promiscuous" points produced by the discarded
\texttt{city\_hint\_only} fallback tier — unrelated location strings (an
Instagram handle, a theatre in Medellín, a Paris address) all resolving
to the same place. Removing that tier, together with a
coordinate-clustering filter flagging any point shared by three or more
distinct location names, left 71 events with a trustworthy pin.

A live query against the current graph supplies a second figure, on date
extraction: of 751 \texttt{:Event} nodes, 6 (0.8\%) carry an
\texttt{eventDate} outside a plausible 1950--2028 range, all through the
mechanism DD-039 identified — a number embedded in the caption read as
the date itself, for instance an event dated 1941 from a caption naming
"Hotel Mandalay 1941" and one dated 2060 from a caption addressing
people "de 18 a 35 años". This is a lower bound: a date that is wrong
but still lands inside the plausible range does not surface here.


% ============================================================================
\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{ch:conclusion}
% ============================================================================

This thesis set out to map, analyse and make discoverable
culturally-relevant activity in the Paris region from public Instagram
data, through the four objectives of \S\ref{sec:objectives}.

\section{Objective 1: Thematic Agendas and Trustworthy Accounts}

The four founding agendas — literature, photography, cinema, theatre —
plus community events, are maintained today through 349 curated accounts
(\S\ref{sec:thematic}). An automated classifier was tried first and
selected a culturally valuable account under 10\% of the time, so hand
curation replaced it as the trust signal (\S\ref{sec:methodology-curation})
— the project's clearest methodological contribution, arrived at by
measuring the alternative rather than assuming it.

\section{Objective 2: An Event-Extraction Pipeline}

Three layers — semantic filtering, event-likelihood classification, LLM
extraction and gating (\S\ref{sec:event-pipeline}) — produce the
751-event catalogue of \S\ref{sec:thematic}. Three rounds of blind
human-versus-pipeline comparison moved agreement from 58\% to 96.7\% and
precision from 56\% to 97.3\% (\S\ref{sec:eval-design}). Photography and
cinema looked under-detected against their curated account base, but the
free-text \texttt{eventArtTags} data (25 Cine, 11 Fotografía mentions
against 2 events each under the earlier count) points to a narrower
measurement window rather than missing activity; literature has a
separate, taxonomy-level gap instead — none of the sixteen event-type
labels corresponds to it at all. Where the pipeline is not reliable
(geocoding, date extraction, LLM classification, \S\ref{sec:limitations})
is reported as part of the same result, not minimised.

\section{Objective 3: Mapping the Network}

Actor categorisation (organisation type, geographic zone, cultural
identity) is answered by the same curated fields as Objective 1
(Appendix~\ref{app:schema}). The structural half — who are the
connective actors — was measured once, early, on a network since changed
by scope and cleanup, and is reported as a closed, dated finding (92.5\%
individual accounts, a small institutional bridging core, some of the
most central accounts carrying almost no content of their own) rather
than revalidated here (\S\ref{sec:future-work-network}).

\section{Objective 4: Enabling Participation}

This objective's success criterion was deliberately human: leading to
actually meeting someone. What exists is the infrastructure toward that
end — a live, bilingual, static site with no backend, no accounts, no
personal data collection, and a documented publication lifecycle (staged
review, ranking, retention, a logged manual deploy;
\S\ref{sec:website}, \S\ref{sec:lifecycle}). Whether it meets the human
criterion remains untested: the site is live, but using it this way is a
distinct act from building it, and that has not yet been attempted.

\section{Scope and Its Cost}
\label{sec:scope-evolution}

The project's inclusion criterion shifted from nationality to cultural
relevance in two places, one visible and one not until measured. The
website's ranking formula had applied a $\times 1.05$ bonus to any event
with a non-null \texttt{culturalIdentity} — a field overwhelmingly
Colombian and Latin American — favouring one set of identities by
default; it was removed, and community detection was confirmed purely
topological, unaffected by the field. Less visibly, the event
detector's reference vocabulary had been written entirely around
Colombian diaspora events: the blind evaluation measured general and
French accounts losing real events at 12.9\% against 4.3\% for Colombian
and Latin American ones, on captions unambiguous to a human reader — the
nationality criterion was encoded in the instrument, not only in the
project's stated scope. A 2026-08-31 snapshot of the 285 curated accounts
confirms the population is now general rather than diaspora-specific
(67.7\% \texttt{culturalIdentity = ``No aplica''}); the diaspora reading
survives as one lens over a subset, not the corpus's boundary.

The recurring lesson underneath all four objectives is that automated
signals fail silently — a geocoder returning a precise, wrong coordinate
rather than none, a language model typing an event "not cultural" while
the surrounding code created it anyway (DD-039) — and were caught by a
human reading output, not by the system flagging itself. Human curation
was the more reliable signal, but does not scale and embeds one
researcher's judgement, which the evaluation protocol was measured
against rather than an external ground truth. The practical response
was to put a deterministic, checkable gate in front of and behind every
probabilistic component, and reserve relevance judgement for a person.

\section{Limitations}

The seven limitations catalogued in \S\ref{sec:limitations} amount to
boundaries of a few kinds: single-platform sourcing makes organisations
that announce elsewhere structurally invisible; the geography is
concentrated but not clean, since institutions physically outside
France enter through curation and automatic discovery alike; several
pipeline components (geocoding, date extraction, LLM classification)
fail silently rather than flagging low confidence; and the evaluation
measures agreement with a single annotator's judgement, not a consensus
ground truth. That last point is also a cost: the corpus's boundary is
set by how much one researcher can review by hand, the direct price of
replacing automated scoring with human curation
(\S\ref{sec:methodology-curation}) — a trade this thesis argues was the
right one on the evidence, but a real one. Every figure in this thesis
is, for the same reason, a snapshot of a graph and a pipeline that were
both still changing as it was written, stated with its run of origin
rather than as a standing fact. To keep Chapter~\ref{ch:results}
reproducible against a fixed target rather than only a moving one, the
export the 751-event figures are drawn from is archived, dated, in the
repository (\texttt{data\_processed/snapshots/data\_2026-08-28.json})
rather than left implicit in a live database that keeps changing after
submission.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future-work}

\subsection{Revisiting Network Analysis on the Curated Graph}
\label{sec:future-work-network}

The account-network analysis described in \S\ref{sec:network-methods}
was run once, at RUN-015 (2026-07-24), against 7{,}269 \texttt{:Account}
nodes, before the scope broadened beyond the Latin American diaspora and
before \texttt{cleanup\_legacy\_accounts.py} removed the uncurated
periphery: a 92.5\% individual-account graph around a small institutional
core, dominated by a single giant component, with some of the highest-
betweenness and highest-PageRank accounts carrying almost no posts of
their own — the clearest evidence in the project's own record for why an
account-selection criterion should not read relevance off an account's
own content. That analysis is closed rather than current, since the
criterion it served has been retired and the graph's scope and
composition have since changed; what remains open, and is left as future
work, is whether the same pattern (a small institutional bridging core
amid a large organic periphery) survives on the curated subgraph as it
stands today, a smaller and cheaper question to ask than the original
one, and worth asking on its own sociological merits rather than as a
selection tool.

\subsection{Pipeline and Product}

Beyond the network-analysis question above, the project's own planning
notes and open items point to a concrete list: a new account classifier
with explicit geography and cultural-relevance dimensions, should account
discovery ever need to scale beyond what manual curation can review; an
expanded seed set from Latin American consulates in Paris (DD-022 lists
the handles, several still unverified); a revised harvest cycle
prioritising cheap profile scraping before expensive post scraping; a
literature label added to the event taxonomy and a backfill decision for
\texttt{eventArtTags} (DD-042), directly motivated by the
under-detection of photography and cinema events noted in
\S\ref{sec:thematic}; resolution of the open question in DD-035 on
ongoing exhibitions; normalisation of stylised Unicode digits in captions
if confirmed frequent (DD-036); the remaining site screens — a
clickable arrondissement and commune map, an explore view, and
organiser-profile pages; a backfill decision for
\texttt{titleFr}/\texttt{descriptionFr} on events created before DD-051,
together with a first quality check of the translations it produces;
whether DeepSeek should remain in the default cloud cascade at all, given
the empty-response failures of DD-050; and collapsing data export, static
page generation and deploy (\S\ref{sec:lifecycle}) into a single command
so publishing stops being the step that gets forgotten (DD-053).

\chapter{Selected Decision Log Entries}
\label{app:decisions}

The full decision log (\texttt{docs/decisions\_es.md}) is written in
Spanish and runs to over 75 entries; it is the project's real-time
engineering diary, not a document written for this thesis. The entries
below are the author's own translations of the ones most load-bearing
for the argument made in the body chapters, condensed for space —
validation minutiae (exact test-case counts, \texttt{py\_compile}
confirmations) are omitted where they do not change the substance of
the decision.

\textbf{DD-006 --- Seed strategy: BFS from a single institutional
anchor (June 2026).} The network was seeded from \texttt{@consuladocolparis}
and expanded via mentions, tags and \texttt{relatedProfiles} rather than
random scraping, on the reasoning that a consulate is the most central
node of the Colombian diaspora in Paris — every relevant account sits
within 1--3 degrees of it. A manually curated list of Colombian accounts
in Paris was considered and rejected for researcher bias and for missing
unexpected connections.

\textbf{DD-026 --- Separating seed role from cultural relevance.} An
earlier decision (DD-016) had put government accounts in a tier excluded
from cultural-relevance analysis, since institutional accounts distort
those rankings; but DD-006 and DD-022 use
exactly those accounts (consulates, embassies) as the structural seed of
the whole discovery process — without them there is no BFS expansion and
no \texttt{relatedProfiles} to follow. The fix separates two questions
that a single criterion had been conflating: whether an account is a
valid cultural-relevance target (no, for consulates and embassies) and
whether it should stay active as a discovery anchor (yes, unconditionally
for this block) — implemented as \texttt{role="seed\_source"} with
\texttt{keep=False} regardless of computed score.

\textbf{DD-031 / DD-032 --- Geographic misclassification via a bounding
box and an Alliance-Française-specific rule.} A bio-text city-name
fallback used to flag accounts outside France caught only two known
cases; auditing raw \texttt{businessAddress} data found four more
accounts with real \texttt{latitude}/\texttt{longitude} outside France
but no diaspora-city text in their bio, invisible to the old fallback.
The fix reads coordinates directly and penalises anything outside a
France bounding box (\texttt{lat 41.0--51.5, lon -5.5--9.7}), which
generalises to any country without a maintained city list — at the
accepted cost of excluding French overseas territories, a deliberate
scope choice given the project's Île-de-France focus. One residual case,
an Alliance Française branch in Medellín whose \texttt{businessAddress}
had no coordinates at all and whose bio mentioned "Francia" as a subject
rather than a location, needed a second, narrower rule keyed to the
username pattern \texttt{alian[cz]a.\{0,4\}frances} combined with a
non-French city token — deliberately not generalised to any diaspora
account with a home-city name in its handle, which would have produced
false positives among legitimate accounts like
\texttt{medellin\_en\_paris}.

\textbf{DD-034 / DD-037 / DD-039 --- Three rounds of date-extraction
bugs.} DD-034 stopped the date parser from inventing a day-of-month when
a caption named only a month, after a comparison against blind human
labels found the pipeline assigning specific calendar days to captions
that only said "this March." DD-037, the first live run of the retuned
pipeline (150 posts, 96.7\% agreement), traced its remaining
disagreements to a theatre-season notation ("26/27") misread as a date
and a 600-character caption-truncation window that discarded dates
appearing later in long flyer-style captions — both fixed the same day.
DD-039, auditing 90 events already written to production, found two
further patterns: events tagged by the language model's own taxonomy as
not a real cultural event were still created, because node-creation
logic checked the invitation flags but never the category label itself;
and a 1{,}095-day date-sanity clamp silently did nothing on three events
whose dates should have been rejected, because a single
\texttt{try/except} wrapped both the event date and the post timestamp
together, so a parse failure on either one skipped the clamp entirely
rather than falling back safely.

\textbf{DD-050 --- DeepSeek's silent empty-content failures reorder the
provider cascade.} A live run of 315 posts through the four-provider
cloud fallback (Groq $\to$ Google $\to$ DeepSeek $\to$ Cerebras) found
DeepSeek failing more often than the other three, but not with a
rate-limit error: it returns HTTP 200 with an empty response body,
which crashes the subsequent JSON parse with a generic error that gave
no hint of the real cause. Since DeepSeek is also the only one of the
four with no free tier, the cascade was reordered to
\texttt{groq $\to$ google $\to$ cerebras $\to$ deepseek}, and the empty-body
case is now detected explicitly rather than left to fail as a generic
parse error.

\textbf{DD-052 --- Filtering pending review by geographic zone.}
Reviewing the first real batch of pending events by hand, the author
found himself rejecting every single "Fuera de Francia" ("outside
France") event one at a time. \texttt{review\_events.py} gained a zone
filter (inherited from the publishing account's own \texttt{geoZone})
and a bulk-reject action over whatever the filter currently shows — with
an explicit, unresolved caveat left in the log: \texttt{geoZone}
describes where the publishing account is based, not necessarily where
the event itself takes place, so the bulk action is a useful heuristic
for the common case that motivated it, not a per-event guarantee.
