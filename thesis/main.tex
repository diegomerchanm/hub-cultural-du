\begin{abstract}
Culturally-relevant activity in the Paris region — with priority given to
photography, cinema, literature, theatre, and locally-organized community
events — is rarely centralized: it lives across hundreds of Instagram
accounts, is announced inconsistently, and is legible mainly to whoever
already follows the right accounts. This project, which originated from
but is no longer limited to the specific case of the Latin American
diaspora in France, builds a pipeline that scrapes Instagram profiles and
posts into a Neo4j graph, applies a three-layer NLP/LLM event-extraction
pipeline validated by three rounds of blind human-versus-pipeline
evaluation (agreement rising from 58\% to 96.7\%, precision from 56\% to
97.3\%), and treats hand curation of accounts — rather than an automated
classifier, which under 10\% of the time selected a culturally valuable
account — as the operative trust signal. A research-operations discipline
(a real-time decision log, a run log, and dry-run-first iteration on a
continuously growing, shared database) is argued to be part of the
methodology rather than incidental project management. The pipeline feeds
a public, bilingual, static discovery website with no backend, no user
accounts and no personal data collection, whose publication cycle —
staged human review of every detected event, a rolling retention window,
and a deliberate, logged manual deploy step — is itself part of what this
thesis reports as method. A byproduct of the graph, exact PageRank and
betweenness centrality over the account network, still feeds one term of
the site's ranking formula and one badge, but is reported here only to
that extent: the account-selection role it was originally built for was
superseded by human curation, and its fuller exploitation is left as
future work. The thesis's contribution is threefold: methodological (human
curation as an evidenced alternative to automated account scoring; a
blind evaluation protocol that repeatedly and measurably drove pipeline
redesign; a transparent account of where automated signals fail silently);
empirical (a mapped, if partial, cultural network with a documented,
improving detection accuracy); and applied (a live tool a member of the
community it describes can actually use). The project's scope itself
evolved, from a nationality-based inclusion criterion to a purely cultural
one, after a blind evaluation showed the earlier criterion was not only a
conceptual boundary but a measurable bias baked into the detection
pipeline's own reference vocabulary.
\end{abstract}

\section{Context and Motivation}
\label{sec:context}

Hub Cultural began as an attempt to make visible something that is well
known anecdotally but poorly documented empirically: the cultural life
that Latin American, and in particular Colombian, communities sustain in
the Paris region through small associations, independent venues, cultural
attachés, and a dense but scattered presence on Instagram. The author's
own Colombian background supplied both the initial motivation and the
first seed account for data collection — the Colombian consulate in
Paris (\texttt{@consuladocolparis}) — from which the network was grown
outward through automatic relationship discovery.

As the project progressed, the boundary that had organized it from the
start — \emph{is this account or event part of the Latin American
diaspora?} — proved both practically brittle and conceptually narrower
than what the data, and the underlying research interest, actually
supported. Many of the best-connected cultural accounts discovered
through the network turned out to be French institutions, mixed-nationality
collectives, or venues with no diaspora affiliation at all, but which were
unmistakably part of the same cultural ecosystem the project set out to
map — programming photography exhibitions, independent cinema, literary
events, and theatre alongside explicitly Colombian or Latin American
programming. The project's scope therefore evolved in two steps: first
from Colombia-specific to Latin-American-diaspora-wide, and subsequently
from a nationality-based criterion to a purely cultural one. The operative
question became not \emph{who is behind this account} but \emph{is this
culturally relevant}, with explicit priority — inherited from the
project's original four thematic agendas (\texttt{docs/objetivos.md}) —
given to photography, cinema, literature, and theatre, alongside a fifth,
less formally bounded category: events organized by and for local
communities, regardless of national or cultural origin.

This evolution is treated in this thesis not as scope creep to be
apologized for, but as a methodological finding in its own right
(\S\ref{sec:scope-evolution}): a nationality-based inclusion criterion
turned out to be both harder to operationalize consistently and less
faithful to how cultural networks actually cohere in practice, where
diaspora and host-society cultural production overlap and reinforce each
other rather than remaining cleanly separable. It also turned out to be
measurably encoded in the pipeline itself: the semantic reference set used
by the first stage of event detection was written entirely in the
vocabulary of Colombian diaspora events, and a blind evaluation of 100
posts showed that non-diaspora cultural accounts consequently lost real
events at roughly three times the rate of diaspora accounts
(\S\ref{sec:eval-design}). The scope question was therefore never purely
conceptual; it had a direct, quantifiable effect on what the system was
able to see.

\section{Problem Statement and Research Question}
\label{sec:research-question}

The cultural activity produced by, and adjacent to, migrant and diaspora
communities in a host country is rarely centralized: it lives across
dozens of Instagram accounts, is announced inconsistently, and is legible
mainly to whoever already follows the right accounts. This is true of
Latin American cultural life in the Paris region specifically, but — as
this project's own data eventually made clear — it is not a problem
unique to any one national community; it is a structural feature of how
small-scale cultural production circulates on social media generally.

This thesis asks: \emph{How is culturally-relevant activity — with
priority given to photography, cinema, literature, theatre, and local
community cultural life — organized as a network of accounts and events
in the Paris region, and can that structure be systematically mapped,
analyzed, and made discoverable using publicly available Instagram
data?}

This breaks into three sub-questions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Structural.} Who are the connective actors in this
    network — which accounts function as hubs or bridges between
    otherwise separate cultural circles, and does this differ between
    institutional accounts and individual or grassroots ones?
    \item \textbf{Thematic.} How is cultural activity distributed across
    the project's priority domains (photography, cinema, literature,
    theatre, community events), and how well does an automated pipeline
    classify it into these domains compared to human judgment?
    \item \textbf{Practical.} Can the resulting graph be turned into a
    public-facing tool that helps the community it describes actually
    discover and attend these events — and what does building that tool
    reveal about the reliability of the underlying data?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Objectives}
\label{sec:objectives}

The project's objectives, as originally framed (\texttt{docs/objetivos.md})
and updated here to reflect the current, cultural-relevance-based scope:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Build and maintain four thematic monitoring agendas —
    \emph{literature}, \emph{photography}, \emph{cinema}, and
    \emph{theatre} — tracking the organizations that play an active role
    in each, complemented by a fifth, less formally bounded priority:
    cultural events organized by and for local communities.
    \item Consolidate a pipeline that extracts events from the accounts
    under these agendas, primarily from Instagram and, prospectively,
    from websites and other social networks.
    \item Map the network of actors within each thematic agenda and
    categorize them — by type of organization, geographic zone, cultural
    identity where relevant, and structural role in the network.
    \item Evaluate the participation opportunities these networks open
    up, using a deliberately human, non-technical success criterion: on
    this dimension, the project succeeds when it leads to actually
    meeting someone through it.
\end{enumerate}

Geographically the project centers on Île-de-France, though a meaningful
share of curated accounts and events originate from, or are organized by,
institutions physically located outside France (\S\ref{sec:limitations}).
Instagram is the sole data source, a deliberate boundary rather than an
oversight: it is the platform where this cultural activity is, in
practice, announced, and only public accounts are in scope. Inclusion is
governed by cultural relevance rather than national origin, with the four
thematic pillars and the community-events category acting as explicit
priorities rather than hard filters.

\section{Research Design Overview}
\label{sec:research-design}

The project is organised as a seven-phase pipeline, each phase
implemented as one or more independent, re-runnable scripts writing to a
shared Neo4j graph:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Extraction} — Instagram profiles and posts are scraped
    through Apify actors into local JSON
    (\texttt{1\_harvest\_ig\_profiles.py},
    \texttt{1\_harvest\_ig\_posts.py}, \texttt{extract\_profiles.py}).
    \item \textbf{Ingestion} — the raw JSON is loaded into Neo4j Aura
    (\texttt{2\_build\_graph.py}).
    \item \textbf{Network analysis} — the graph is exported to CSV and
    analysed offline with igraph and leidenalg, and the resulting metrics
    are written back (\texttt{3\_analyze\_network.py}).
    \item \textbf{Manual categorisation} — a hand-curated spreadsheet of
    account attributes is uploaded onto the matching \texttt{:Account}
    nodes (\texttt{load\_manual\_account\_categorization.py}).
    \item \textbf{Event enrichment} — posts are passed through the
    three-layer detection pipeline to create \texttt{:Event} nodes, with
    inline deduplication (\texttt{4\_enrich\_events\_extract.py}), followed
    by a standalone deduplication pass over existing events
    (\texttt{4\_enrich\_events\_resolve.py}).
    \item \textbf{Geographic enrichment} — location strings are geocoded
    and placed in an administrative hierarchy
    (\texttt{4\_enrich\_locations.py}).
    \item \textbf{Cleanup} — accounts never touched by manual curation,
    and everything depending exclusively on them, are deleted
    (\texttt{cleanup\_legacy\_accounts.py}).
\end{enumerate}

Two design principles run through all seven phases and are treated in
this thesis as methodological rather than merely technical.
\emph{Idempotency}: every script can be re-run without duplicating or
corrupting what previous runs produced, because each checks a sentinel
property before processing (\texttt{eventExtracted}, \texttt{lat}) or
merges rather than inserts. \emph{Separation of provenance}: the manual
curation phase deliberately writes to a property namespace
(\texttt{artType}, \texttt{culturalIdentity}, \texttt{geoZone},
\texttt{manualFollowersCount}) disjoint from the one the ingestion phase
writes, so that neither can silently overwrite the other. The practical
consequence is that automated and human-produced data remain
distinguishable in the graph after the fact, which is what makes the
curation-as-trust-signal argument of \S\ref{sec:methodology-curation}
verifiable rather than merely asserted.

\section{Research Operations: Decision Logging, Run Tracking, and Iterative Safety}
\label{sec:research-ops}

Independently of the technical pipeline itself, this project maintained
three interlocking practices for managing the research process, treated
here as a methodological contribution rather than incidental project
management. Together they make the pipeline's evolution auditable after
the fact — which is not free in a setting where the object of study is a
continuously growing graph, reprocessed repeatedly by successive versions
of the extraction logic.

\textbf{A real-time decision log.} Every non-trivial design choice was
recorded, at the moment it was made, in a sequentially numbered decision
log (\texttt{docs/decisions\_es.md}, fifty-one entries numbered DD-001 to
DD-053; two numbers in that range, DD-021 and DD-024, were reserved for
decisions referenced in the code but never written up) rather than
reconstructed retrospectively for this thesis.\footnote{The log is maintained in Spanish, the language in
which the project work was conducted; \texttt{docs/decisions.md} holds a
partial English translation that currently stops at DD-033. Where entries
are quoted in this thesis, the translation is the author's.} Each entry
states the alternatives considered, the evidence that motivated the final
choice, and, where applicable, validation results. Four entries illustrate
the range of what this captured. DD-041 documents a geographic-conflict
guardrail added to the event-deduplication resolver after a production
dry-run surfaced a specific failure mode: two structurally similar events
merged despite being in different countries. It was validated first against
fourteen isolated test cases and then in a live dry-run over the 664 events
then in the graph, in which it blocked six geographically contradictory
merges while leaving intact the 68 duplicate pairs the run had identified
for merging. DD-042 documents the addition of a per-event, LLM-generated
tag field, designed specifically to avoid a parsing failure present in an
earlier, account-level free-text field, and scoped explicitly as
prospective — the events already in the graph were left without it rather
than backfilled at token cost. DD-043 and DD-044 document the design of the
Neo4j-to-static-JSON export and the resulting public website, including an
explicit accounting of which ranking components could be precomputed at
export time and which had to remain client-side because they depend on the
current date or on the individual visitor's session. Maintaining this log
in real time, rather than writing it up after the fact, is itself a
methodological choice: it captures the reasoning available \emph{at the
time} of each decision, including alternatives that were considered and
rejected — something retrospective documentation systematically loses.

\textbf{A run log.} Separately, every substantive execution of the
pipeline against live data — not every script invocation, but every run
whose output was retained or acted upon — was logged with its date,
parameters, and a summary of results (\texttt{docs/runs\_log\_es.md},
RUN-001 to RUN-021 at the time of writing). This served two purposes
beyond record-keeping. It made it possible to distinguish a genuine
change in the underlying data (new accounts or events discovered) from a
change in pipeline logic when comparing two exports — a distinction that
becomes impossible to reconstruct once several months of both have
accumulated. And it created a paper trail for decisions that depended on
a specific run's output rather than on memory: the decision not to add a
venue-specificity guardrail to the event resolver, for instance, is
recorded in RUN-021 with the concrete case that prompted it (one event
node absorbing three separate watch parties for the same football match,
held in different Paris bars) and the reasoning for accepting it.

\textbf{Dry-run-first, diagnose-first design.} Every script that writes
to the live Neo4j graph was built with a \texttt{--dry-run} or
\texttt{--diagnose} mode as a first-class feature, not an afterthought:
\texttt{cleanup\_legacy\_accounts.py} runs its deletion logic inside a
single transaction and issues a \texttt{ROLLBACK} under
\texttt{--dry-run}, so a preview's reported counts are exact rather than
estimated; \texttt{load\_manual\_account\_categorization.py} and the
event-extraction and event-resolution scripts all follow the same
pattern. Combined with the idempotent design of the extraction and
ingestion scripts, this made it possible to iterate on pipeline logic
against a live, shared, non-trivially-sized research database without the
usual risk of an irreversible mistake. The discipline was not
self-enforcing, however, and DD-038 records what happens when it lapses:
the diagnostic CSV export was gated on \texttt{--dry-run}, so the first
real production run of the event extractor produced no comparison file
and — because event timestamps were written as strings rather than native
Neo4j datetimes — could not be reliably queried by date afterwards
either. A run that worked correctly left almost no auditable trace. The
fix restored parity between the two modes, on the principle that a
production run needs to be at least as inspectable as a rehearsal.

Together, these three practices form a coherent research-operations
discipline: decisions are recorded when made, runs are recorded when
executed, and changes to the live data are always previewable before
being committed. For a thesis built on a continuously evolving dataset
rather than a fixed one, this discipline is what makes it possible to
state, for any given result in Chapter~\ref{ch:results}, exactly which
version of the pipeline and which run produced it.

\section{Data Collection}
\label{sec:data-collection}

Data collection relies on Apify's hosted \texttt{instagram-profile-scraper}
and \texttt{instagram-post-scraper} actors rather than on a self-maintained
browser-automation stack (DD-002). The reasoning recorded at the time was
one of maintenance cost and risk: Instagram actively blocks direct
scraping, so a self-hosted scraper implies continuous maintenance and
IP-blocking risk, whereas a hosted actor externalises both at a metered
per-use price. That price is not incidental to the method — it is tracked
per run in \texttt{.apify\_cost\_log.json} and functions as a real
constraint on sampling decisions, which is why the harvesting logic is
built around spending as little as possible per unit of new information.
Profile scraping and post scraping are separate scripts (DD-003) because
they use different actors, return different JSON structures, and need
different refresh frequencies.

Seed selection changed across the project's two phases. The first phase
used a single seed, the Colombian consulate in Paris, and expanded outward
breadth-first through mentions, tags and Instagram's own
\texttt{relatedProfiles} suggestions (DD-006); the reasoning was that a
consulate is the most central institutional node of the community it
serves, so relevant accounts should lie within one to three hops, and that
organic expansion avoids the researcher-bias of a hand-written list. The
second phase replaced the single seed with curated seed files
(\texttt{config/seeds\_*.json}) covering Latin American consulates and
embassies in France plus French cultural and academic institutions
connected to Latin America (DD-022). These institutional seeds raised a
conflict the log resolves explicitly: the account-classification scheme
treated government accounts as out of cultural scope, yet the entire
discovery process depends on them. DD-026 separates the two questions —
\emph{is this account a valid target for cultural analysis?} and \emph{is
this account structurally necessary to discover the network?} — and
assigns consulates the role of seed source without treating them as
cultural targets.

Post collection is deliberately recency-biased rather than
density-maximising. Rather than retrieving a fixed number of historical
posts per account, the harvester computes a per-account time window equal
to the gap since that account's most recent known post, capped at a
configurable maximum, and merges results into a rolling window of the
fifty most recent posts (DD-028, DD-029). The rationale is that the
downstream objective is detecting forthcoming events, not reconstructing
publication history; the earlier fixed-window design had a blind spot in
which an account with a recent post was skipped entirely, losing anything
published in between. A related robustness fix is worth recording as
method rather than trivia: the Apify actor signals "no content found" by
returning a one-element list containing an \texttt{error} key, which is
truthy in Python and was therefore silently treated as a successful
single-post result, corrupting both the run counters and — before the
merge logic existed — the stored file (DD-030). Data collection failures
in this setting do not always announce themselves as failures.

\section{Data Model}
\label{sec:data-model}

The graph uses eleven node labels and sixteen relationship types. Accounts,
posts, hashtags, locations, music tracks, comments and IGTV videos come
from ingestion; events come from the NLP pipeline; cities, arrondissements
and countries come from geocoding. Accounts additionally carry
\texttt{:Public} or \texttt{:Private} as a second label rather than a
boolean property (DD-005), which allows indexed filtering in Cypher and
visual separation in the Neo4j browser. The full schema is reproduced in
Appendix~\ref{app:schema}.

The design principle behind the node set (DD-004) is that the model should
capture three distinct dimensions at once: content (posts, hashtags),
social interaction (mentions, tags, co-authorship), and cultural context
(locations, music tracks). A minimal account-and-post model was considered
and rejected on the grounds that it would discard exactly the signals the
research question depends on — venues, collaborations and thematic
markers. One consequence of this model matters for the analysis chapter
and is easy to overlook: social relationships such as \texttt{MENTIONS}
and \texttt{TAGS\_USER} originate at the \texttt{:Post} node, not at the
account, so any account-to-account social network has to be obtained by
projecting author$\to$post$\to$target rather than read directly off the
stored edges (\S\ref{sec:network-methods}).

\section{Network Analysis: Status and Scope in This Thesis}
\label{sec:network-methods}

An early phase of the project treated graph-theoretic analysis of the
account network — exact PageRank and betweenness centrality, Leiden
community detection, k-core decomposition, participation coefficient and
E-I index, computed offline with igraph and leidenalg over two separate
network layers because Neo4j's Graph Data Science library was unavailable
in this environment (DD-013) — as a central method and, through an
automated classifier built on top of it, as the intended criterion for
deciding which accounts belonged in the corpus at all. That role did not
survive contact with the data: \S\ref{sec:methodology-curation} reports
that under 10\% of the accounts the classifier selected turned out to be
culturally valuable on manual review, and hand curation replaced it as
the operative trust signal. Community detection, modularity and
brokerage analysis are consequently no longer part of this project's
active methodology, and are not reported as findings in
Chapter~\ref{ch:results}; the full design, together with the one
substantive result it produced before being superseded — that some of
the most structurally central accounts in the network had almost no
content of their own, which is direct evidence for why a content-based
selection criterion was the wrong one — is kept as a documented but
closed line of work in \S\ref{sec:future-work-network}.

One artefact of that earlier work remains live rather than archived: the
account-level PageRank and betweenness values it computed are still read
by the site's ranking formula (\S\ref{sec:ranking}, term $A$) and drive a
single interface badge marking structurally bridging accounts. That
residual dependency, and the caveat it carries — the values are not
recomputed on an ongoing basis — is documented where it actually matters,
alongside the formula itself, rather than here.

\section{Event Detection Pipeline}
\label{sec:event-pipeline}

Event detection runs in three layers over post captions. The design was
originally four layers and was reduced to three in August 2026 after the
evaluation described in \S\ref{sec:eval-design}.

\textbf{Layer 1 — semantic candidate filtering.} Each caption is embedded
with \texttt{paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM-L12-v2} and compared by cosine
similarity against a reference set of roughly 100 phrases covering
several event types, three languages and six announcement forms (calls,
openings, listings, date-plus-place, registration, reminders). Embeddings
rather than keywords were chosen because the corpus mixes Spanish, French
and English with emoji and slang, where keyword matching fails on
orthographic variation and indirect phrasing (DD-007); the reference set
is large rather than minimal (DD-010) because a narrow reference space
misses exactly the small community events the project most wants to
capture. Layer 1 exists primarily as a cost control: running the NLI
model over every post was measured at five to seven hours on the
available hardware, whereas pre-filtering reduces the candidate set to
roughly 40\% of the corpus and the total to about eighteen minutes
(DD-009).

\textbf{Layer 2 — is this event-like at all.} Surviving candidates are
passed to a binary natural-language-inference classifier
(\texttt{MoritzLaurer/multilingual-MiniLMv2-L6-mnli-xnli}). Two
decisions here are worth stating explicitly. The model is multilingual by
requirement, not by preference: an earlier English-trained NLI model
produced a detection rate of 0.7\% on Spanish and French captions —
effectively noise (DD-008). And the hypothesis sentence is now formulated
in the caption's own detected language, after a fixed-Spanish hypothesis
was shown to depress scores for English and French captions regardless of
their content.

\textbf{Layer 3 — LLM extraction, typing and gating.} Candidates that
pass Layer 2 are sent to a large language model — Ollama locally, or one
of four cloud providers tried in order with automatic failover and a cap
on rate-limit waiting (Groq, Google, Cerebras, DeepSeek, DD-033) — which
extracts title, description, date, location and price range in Spanish,
together with French translations of the title and description generated
in the same call at no extra request cost (\texttt{title\_fr},
\texttt{description\_fr}, DD-051, feeding the bilingual site of
\S\ref{sec:website}), assigns an event type from a fixed sixteen-label
taxonomy, proposes one to three free-form artistic tags (DD-042), and —
the decisive change — \emph{gates} node creation. If the model reports
that the post is not a public invitation, or not upcoming, no
\texttt{:Event} node is created at all. In the earlier design the node
was created regardless and merely scored lower, which
\S\ref{sec:eval-design} shows was the single largest source of false
positives.

DeepSeek sits last in that order rather than third, and the reordering
is itself evidence about a failure mode worth recording. In its original
position it failed more often than the three free-tier providers, but
not through the expected quota error: it returned HTTP 200 with an empty
\texttt{message.content}, which the JSON parser downstream treated
identically to a malformed response until an explicit emptiness check
was added to tell the two failure modes apart (DD-050). Because DeepSeek
is the only one of the four that is paid, and proved the least stable of
the four across a live run of 315 posts, it was moved behind the three
free providers so that it is tried only once they are all exhausted,
rather than ahead of one of them for no reason the observed failure
pattern could justify.

Three further constraints shape the layer-3 stage. Calls are deduplicated
by post identifier within a run, so co-published posts share one call.
Reasoning traces are requested only in dry-run mode, since they are
expensive and only useful for auditing. And a deterministic
\texttt{has\_text\_date} gate — requiring that a date actually appear in
the caption text — is evaluated \emph{before} the model is called rather
than after, which was measured to remove 40.7\% of calls offline and
48.3\% in production without changing a single final decision (DD-036).
The date extractor behind that gate is itself constrained to avoid
manufacturing precision: a month or year mentioned without a day is
discarded rather than resolved to an arbitrary first-of-month (DD-034),
after four disagreements in the evaluation sample were traced to exactly
that behaviour.

Newly created events inherit \texttt{artType}, \texttt{institutionType},
\texttt{culturalIdentity}, \texttt{geoZone} and \texttt{parentInstitution}
directly from the publishing account's manual curation, at no model cost,
whenever that account has been curated. Deduplication happens twice: an
inline resolver checks cosine similarity above 0.82 together with a
location match and a date within three days before creating a new node
rather than enriching an existing one, and a standalone resolver
(\S\ref{sec:event-resolver}) runs afterwards over the accumulated set.

\section{Event Deduplication}
\label{sec:event-resolver}

Several accounts routinely publish about the same event, so deduplication
is not an edge case but a structural feature of the corpus. The original
resolver grouped events by normalised location string and compared pairs
only within a group, using date proximity and cosine similarity as
secondary criteria (DD-011). An audit of a production run showed this
ordering to be the wrong way round: two posts about the same concert
written the venue address differently and therefore never met, and an
event with no location string at all could never be compared with
anything (DD-040).

The redesign inverts the order. All pairs are compared by embedding
similarity first, using a vectorised cosine matrix, with dates as
mandatory confirmation rather than as a primary filter: where both dates
exist they must fall within three days and similarity must exceed 0.75;
where one is missing, a stricter similarity threshold of 0.85 compensates
for the lost corroboration. Location was explicitly \emph{not} converted
into an embedding, on the reasoning that text embeddings capture lexical
rather than geographic similarity — two different theatres sharing the
words "Théâtre de" may embed close together, while two addresses on the
same street may not. An audit of the location strings then in the corpus
supported demoting them: roughly a fifth carried a full address, over
half only a venue name, a tenth only a city or country, and around a
seventh were empty.

The predictable cost of dropping the location filter appeared on the
first live dry-run: merges that were geographically contradictory, such as
a Paris event merging with a Madrid one, because generic community posts
share vocabulary regardless of where they take place. The response
(DD-041) was a deliberately small gazetteer of country and city names
drawn from the actual failure cases, which blocks a merge only when both
sides name a recognisable and non-overlapping country. It is permissive
by design — insufficient evidence on either side never blocks — precisely
to avoid reintroducing the exact-match problem the redesign removed. Its
residual limit is documented rather than patched: it compares countries,
so two different venues within the same city can still merge, which
RUN-021 shows happening and which the run log records as an accepted
trade-off for the case at hand.

\section{Evaluation Design: Blind Human-versus-Pipeline Comparison}
\label{sec:eval-design}

The pipeline's accuracy was assessed by blind manual classification of
post captions against the pipeline's own decisions, repeated at three
increasing scales, with each round feeding concrete changes back into the
code. In each round the captions were classified without access to the
pipeline's decision columns, and only then joined against the diagnostic
CSV the extractor emits.

\textbf{Round 1 — 100 posts (2026-08-09).} Overall agreement was 58\%.
The asymmetry mattered more than the headline: 35 disagreements were
posts the pipeline called events and the human classification did not,
against 7 in the opposite direction. Of those 35 false positives, 29
already carried the layer-3 model's own verdict that the post was neither
a public invitation nor upcoming — the system had the information and
did not act on it, because node creation was not conditioned on those
flags, only scoring was. On this sample, the pipeline's actual behaviour
(45 true positives against 80 created nodes and 52 real events, recall
0.865, precision 0.5625) scores F1 = 0.682; simulating the gate raised
precision from 56\% to 90\% and overall agreement from 58\% to 86\%. This
single finding is what turned gating from a scoring adjustment into a
creation condition (\S\ref{sec:event-pipeline}).

The same round produced the bias finding referred to in
\S\ref{sec:context}. The reference phrases of layer 1 had been written
entirely in the vocabulary of Colombian diaspora events. Splitting the
sample by account theme, the false-negative rate was 4.3\% for
Colombian and Latin American accounts against 12.9\% for general and
French accounts — real events with explicit dates and venues, in French
and English, discarded just below the similarity threshold. A criterion
that was intended to be about cultural relevance was, at the level of
implementation, partly about national vocabulary.

\textbf{Round 2 — 495 posts (batches through 2026-08-12).} Aggregate
precision was 0.824 and recall 0.858 (F1 = 0.841). Three code changes followed
(DD-036): eighteen additional reference phrases in an institutional and
formal register, added after the three layer-1 false negatives in the
sample were found to share a sober institutional tone absent from the
reference space; the deterministic \texttt{has\_text\_date} gate, which
was shown offline to block 15 of the 27 false positives in the full
sample; and an explicit negative instruction in the layer-3 prompt
excluding course registrations, enrolment and admission calls, which had
been producing events with a structure indistinguishable from real ones.
A fourth change was considered and rejected on the evidence: lowering the
layer-1 threshold from 0.45 to 0.35 would have recovered seven real
events at the cost of admitting 47 additional non-events, a
signal-to-noise ratio of roughly 1:6.7, which is the wrong trade in a
pipeline whose dominant error was already precision.

\textbf{Round 3 — 150 posts in a live dry-run (2026-08-12, RUN-020).}
Agreement reached 96.7\%, with precision 97.3\% and recall approximately
90\% (F1 $\approx$ 0.935) — the first round run against the modified
pipeline rather than a simulation. Four of the five remaining disagreements were traced to
specific lines of code and fixed the same day (DD-037): a theatre
season written "26/27" parsed as a day-and-month date, and a
600-character truncation window in the date extractor that discarded
dates appearing later in the long multi-line captions typical of event
flyers. The fifth had no identifiable code cause — two nearly identical
posts about the same event, one accepted and one rejected by the language
model — and is recorded as observed variability rather than resolved.

Two methodological caveats belong with these numbers. First, the human
labels are the author's own, so this measures agreement with a single
annotator's criterion, not with a consensus ground truth; the criterion
itself had to be adjudicated in at least one case, where exhibitions
announced with an explicit closing date were ruled to count as events
even when the surrounding text is retrospective (DD-035), and that
adjudication remains flagged as unresolved against the pipeline's own
behaviour. Second, agreement rose across the three rounds partly because
the pipeline improved and partly because the samples differ in
composition, so the figures track a trajectory rather than three
measurements of the same quantity.

\section{Human Curation as the Trust Signal}
\label{sec:methodology-curation}

The project's original design selected accounts automatically. A
classifier scored each discovered account on geographic and cultural
dimensions, combining semantic similarity against reference embeddings
with hard signals from the profile, and accounts above a threshold were
kept. In practice, well under 10\% of the accounts it selected turned out
to be culturally valuable on manual review. The response was not to tune
the classifier further but to change what the pipeline treats as
authoritative: the property \texttt{manualDataCuratedAt}, written only by
\texttt{load\_manual\_account\_categorization.py} when an account appears
in the hand-curated spreadsheet, became the operative signal for "this
account is worth keeping", and the automated score was retired from that
role.

This is a pivot worth stating as a finding rather than as a confession.
Three mechanisms visible in the project's own records explain why the
automated criterion underperformed. First, the score rewarded signals
that correlate with visibility rather than with cultural relevance, so
commercial and institutional accounts with strong profile metadata scored
well while small collectives with sparse profiles did not. Second, the
geographic component was systematically wrong in a way that was invisible
until profiles were inspected by hand: several accounts scored as
France-based were physically located elsewhere, and the fix required
reading latitude and longitude out of the business address and testing
them against a bounding box (DD-031), followed by a further narrow rule
for a residual class the bounding box could not catch — Alliance
Française branches in Latin American cities, whose bios legitimately
mention France as a subject rather than a location (DD-032). Third, and
most tellingly, an account's structural importance in the network is not
the same quantity as its content volume: the one-time network analysis of
\S\ref{sec:future-work-network} found accounts ranking among the top
bridging and top PageRank actors while having almost no posts loaded,
their centrality coming entirely from being mentioned by others. Any
selection criterion that reads relevance off an account's own output will
miss these.

Curation also had to be reconciled with discovery, since the ingestion
step creates one account node per Instagram \texttt{relatedProfiles}
suggestion on every scrape. Treating "not curated" as "delete" would have
destroyed fresh discoveries alongside old noise. The cleanup script
therefore tags, rather than deletes, any uncurated account reachable via
\texttt{RELATED\_TO} from a curated one, marking it
\texttt{discoveredViaCuratedAccount} with a pending review status, so
that these accounts survive as a review queue. The cost of the approach
is stated plainly in \S\ref{sec:limitations}: human curation does not
scale, and the boundary of the corpus is therefore set by how much
reviewing one researcher can do.

\section{Geospatial Enrichment}
\label{sec:geo}

Location strings extracted by the event pipeline are geocoded with
Nominatim, the OpenStreetMap geocoder, chosen because it is free,
requires no API key, covers both France and Colombia adequately, and
imposes a rate limit (one request per second, implemented here as 1.1
seconds between requests) that is tolerable at this corpus size (DD-018).
Resolved locations are placed in an administrative hierarchy —
\texttt{Location} $\to$ \texttt{Arrondissement} $\to$ \texttt{City}
$\to$ \texttt{Country} — with Paris arrondissements derived from postal
codes in the 750XX range. Sessions are logged so that a partial run can
be resumed rather than repeated.

The methodologically interesting part is what happens when geocoding
fails. Roughly one event in seven never acquires coordinates, for two
distinct reasons that should not be conflated: some have no location
text in the caption at all, and some have text that Nominatim cannot
resolve. A fallback to a city-level hint was available and was rejected
as a display strategy, because it produces coordinates that look precise
and are wrong — an event placed at the centre of Paris because its
address could not be parsed is indistinguishable, on a map, from an event
that really is there. The decision taken (\S\ref{sec:website}) is that
the discovery site shows only events with genuinely resolved
coordinates. This is a presentation filter, not a deletion: the
\texttt{:Event} node remains in the graph for analysis, it simply does
not enter a product whose central organising principle is geography.

\section{Ranking and Relevance Scoring}
\label{sec:ranking}

The discovery website ranks events with an explicit, documented formula
rather than an opaque model, on the stated principle of contextual
algorithmic curation rather than personalisation: there are no user
accounts, and the same conditions produce the same ordering for every
visitor. All component signals are converted to percentile ranks over the
universe of publishable events before being combined. The score is

\[
\text{Relevance} = 100 \cdot \left(0.30\,Q + 0.22\,A + 0.18\,B
+ 0.20\,T + 0.10\,C\right) \cdot P
\]

where $Q$ is detection quality ($0.70 \cdot \texttt{eventScore} + 0.30
\cdot \texttt{confidence}$); $A$ is the authority of the publishing
account in the network, itself a weighted blend of PageRank (0.40),
betweenness (0.25), follower count (0.20), participation coefficient
(0.10) and verified status (0.05); $B$ is social resonance (hotness score
and post count); $T$ is temporal proximity, implemented as explicit
steps rather than an exponential decay so that it can be read and
defended; and $C$ is session context. $P$ is a multiplicative penalty or
bonus: political accounts $\times 0.55$, low-confidence extractions
$\times 0.80$, free events $\times 1.05$.

Three aspects of this formula are methodological choices rather than
tuning. First, follower count is deliberately the smallest term in $A$:
a consulate with forty thousand followers is not thereby more culturally
relevant than a collective with three thousand, and PageRank is weighted
highest precisely because it measures recognition \emph{within the
cultural network} rather than general reach. Second, $Q$, $A$, $B$ and
$P$ are precomputed at export time because they require percentile
context over the whole dataset, whereas $T$ and $C$ are computed in the
browser on each visit because they depend on the current date and on the
individual session — precomputing them would freeze the ranking to the
last export (DD-043, DD-044). Third, and discussed at length in
\S\ref{sec:scope-evolution}, an earlier version of the formula included a
$\times 1.05$ bonus for any event carrying a non-null
\texttt{culturalIdentity} value; it was removed when the project's scope
broadened. A mandatory diversification pass then caps how many events
from the same author, category or parent institution can appear together
at the top of the home page, without which the largest category
mechanically dominates the front page.

A caveat belongs here rather than only in \S\ref{sec:network-methods}:
the PageRank and betweenness values behind $A$ are not recomputed on a
running basis. They are read off whichever account happened to be
covered by the last executed pass of the offline network analysis, so an
account curated afterward, or a shift in who mentions whom since that
pass, is not reflected in $A$ until the analysis is deliberately re-run.
This is a live product dependency on a component this thesis otherwise
treats as a closed, superseded line of work
(\S\ref{sec:methodology-curation}); it is reported honestly here rather
than smoothed over, precisely because it is the one place where that
earlier work still has a measurable effect on what a visitor sees.

\section{Publication Lifecycle and Event Retirement}
\label{sec:lifecycle}

Detection and ranking are only two stages of a longer cycle that this
thesis treats as methodology rather than as an implementation footnote,
because it is what makes the published catalogue trustworthy over time
rather than only accurate at the moment of extraction. Three mechanisms
close that loop, in the order an event actually passes through them.

\textbf{Staged human review.} Every \texttt{:Event} node created by the
extraction pipeline (\S\ref{sec:event-pipeline}) is tagged
\texttt{:PendingReview} at the moment it is created, and both the JSON
export that feeds the website and the Excel export used internally
exclude that label by default. Concretely, this means the layer-3 gate
(\S\ref{sec:event-pipeline}) is a necessary but not sufficient condition
for publication: passing it only qualifies an event to enter a review
queue, not to appear on the site. A small local application
(\texttt{review\_events.py}) lets the researcher approve, edit or reject
each pending event; rejection is deliberately soft — it adds a
\texttt{:Rejected} label rather than deleting the node — so that no
review decision is irreversible (\S\ref{sec:website}, DD-047). This
reuses the same label-based status pattern already used for the
automated gate rather than introducing a second status system for human
decisions, and it is the direct methodological answer to
\S\ref{sec:data-quality}'s finding that twelve of twenty
highest-resonance institutional events were, on manual inspection, either
not events at all or misclassified: automated gating alone was not
sufficient to keep the published catalogue clean, and a second,
human-executed gate was added downstream of it rather than tuning the
first one further.

\textbf{A rolling retention window, not deletion.} Consistent with the
project's broader practice of never deleting research data outright
(rejection above, and the researcher-only tier of the Excel export),
\texttt{:Event} nodes are never removed from the graph once approved.
Instead, retention is enforced only at export time: the export query
excludes any event more than a fixed number of days in the past (thirty
by default, configurable per run), and the website's own client-side
logic enforces the same cutoff a second time as a deliberate redundancy,
so that a stale export cannot silently resurrect old events even if the
cutoff at generation time were ever bypassed. The researcher-facing Excel
export is exempt by design: it remains the full historical archive for
analysis, while the public site only ever shows what is current. Before
this cutoff existed, 71\% of parseable events in the graph were already
more than thirty days old and every one of them was still being shipped
to every visitor; the fix is a filter on read, not a change to what is
stored, which keeps the analytical record in \S\ref{ch:results} intact
regardless of what the live site happens to be showing on a given day.

\textbf{Publishing is a deliberate, separate step from generating data.}
Regenerating the exported JSON file, regenerating the static
search-engine-facing pages built from it (\S\ref{sec:website}), and
actually deploying the site to its hosting platform are three distinct
manual commands, none of which triggers another. This was not the
original design intent so much as a gap discovered in production: an
approved batch of events did not appear on the live site because the
data had been re-exported but never redeployed, and the site did not
look broken, only stale, which is precisely why the gap went unnoticed
for a period (DD-053). The lesson recorded alongside the fix is general
rather than specific to this project: a pipeline stage that succeeds
silently and a pipeline stage that was never run produce an
indistinguishable symptom — nothing changes — unless the two are made
observably different, and collapsing the three steps into one command is
listed as future work (\S\ref{sec:future-work}) for exactly that reason.

\section{Ethical Considerations}
\label{sec:ethics}

The corpus is restricted to public accounts and publicly visible posts;
private accounts are labelled as such in the graph and are not scraped.
This does not by itself settle the ethics, since public availability and
consent to research use are different things. Three positions were taken
in consequence. Organisational accounts — venues, institutions,
collectives — are treated as public actors and named directly, since
their posts are promotional by intent and naming them is what makes the
mapping useful to the community. Individuals appearing incidentally in
captions, comments or tags are not the object of analysis, and the
project's data-retention policy works in their favour: the cleanup phase
deletes accounts never touched by manual curation together with their
posts and comments, so the accumulated periphery of individual accounts
discovered through mentions is removed rather than retained
indefinitely.

The public artifact adds a second set of considerations, handled by
design rather than by policy. The site stores no personal data on any
server: it is fully static, session preferences live in the browser's
\texttt{localStorage} under a single key with no third-party cookies and
no personally identifying information, and a reset control is exposed in
the interface. Event cards link back to the original Instagram post
rather than reproducing it, and the site presents itself as a discovery
tool rather than a ticketing service. Automated detection is disclosed on
each event, which matters because a language model sits in the pipeline
and, as \S\ref{sec:limitations} documents, is wrong a measurable share of
the time.

\section{Limitations of Method}
\label{sec:limitations}

The limitations below are drawn from failures actually observed in this
project's runs, not from a generic list of caveats.

\textbf{Geocoding produces false precision when it fails.} Around one
event in seven has no usable coordinates, and the available fallback —
resolving to a city-level hint — yields plausible-looking but wrong
positions. The mitigation is exclusion from the map rather than
approximation (\S\ref{sec:geo}), which trades coverage for honesty.

\textbf{Date extraction is the pipeline's most fragile component.}
Successive rounds of evaluation found dates invented from a bare month
(DD-034), a theatre season "26/27" parsed as a calendar date, dates lost
because the extractor truncated captions at 600 characters (DD-037), and
implausible dates surviving a clamp that was silently skipped whenever
either of two date parses threw — producing events dated 2036, 2052 and
2090 from text that mentioned an anniversary, an age limit and a duration
in minutes (DD-039). Each was fixed once found; the pattern worth
reporting is that all of them failed silently, and all were found by
manual inspection rather than by any automated check.

\textbf{LLM classification is inconsistent at the margin.} RUN-020
recorded two nearly identical posts about the same event receiving
opposite verdicts, with no identifiable code cause. Layer 3 is also
capable of typing an event with a label meaning "not a cultural event"
while the surrounding logic created the node anyway, because creation did
not consult the category field (DD-039).

\textbf{Instagram-only sourcing bounds what can be observed.}
Organisations that announce primarily through mailing lists, their own
websites or other platforms are absent by construction, and the
platform's own visibility dynamics shape which accounts are discoverable
through \texttt{relatedProfiles} at all. The algorithmic layer of the
network is a proprietary artefact and is analysed separately for that
reason (\S\ref{sec:network-methods}).

\textbf{Curation does not scale, and the tier labels are sparse.} The
trust signal that replaced automated scoring is one researcher's
judgement. The consequence is visible in the network analysis: at the
time of RUN-015 only 62 of 7{,}269 account nodes carried an explicit tier
label, so tier-based comparisons describe a small hand-curated subset
rather than the graph as a whole, and this must be stated wherever such
comparisons appear.

\textbf{Geographic scope leaks through automatic discovery.} Accounts
outside the intended geography enter the graph through
\texttt{relatedProfiles} before curation catches them — Alliance
Française branches in Colombian cities being the recurring case (DD-032)
— and at least one event in the institutional sample examined in
\S\ref{sec:data-quality} was a restaurant menu in Spain with no
connection to the project at all.

\textbf{A property inherited from the account is used as a proxy for the
event's own location.} Every \texttt{:Event} node inherits
\texttt{geoZone} from the account that published it, at no additional
extraction cost, and that inheritance has since become an operational
filter: the human-review interface groups pending events by it for bulk
triage (\S\ref{sec:website}, DD-052). A Paris-based account can announce
something happening elsewhere, however, so the property describes who
posted, not necessarily where the event is. That the first bulk-filtered
batch matched the reviewer's manual judgement in practice is evidence
the heuristic works for the common case, not evidence that it is correct
for every event it groups.

\chapter{System Implementation: The Hub Cultural Platform}
\label{ch:implementation}
% ============================================================================

\section{Pipeline Architecture Overview}

The system is a set of independent command-line scripts sharing a Neo4j
Aura database, rather than an orchestrated application. Each script owns
one phase (\S\ref{sec:research-design}), reads what it needs from the
graph or from disk, and writes back a disjoint set of properties. There
is no scheduler and no continuous integration: runs are manual and
deliberate, which suits a research setting where each execution costs
money (Apify credits, LLM tokens) and where the point of a run is usually
to test a change rather than to keep a service fresh.

Two properties of this architecture are load-bearing. \emph{Incremental
processing}: \texttt{extract\_profiles.py} queries the graph for accounts
not yet scraped rather than working from a static list, and the NLP
phases skip any node whose sentinel property is already set, so a
re-run costs nothing for work already done and an interrupted run can
simply be restarted — which is how the connection failure mid-way through
RUN-021 was recovered. \emph{Namespace separation}: manual curation and
automated ingestion write property names chosen not to collide, so
re-running either never clobbers the other's data.

\section{Extraction and Ingestion}

Extraction writes one JSON file per profile into a gitignored
\texttt{data\_raw/} directory; ingestion reads that directory and merges
its contents into the graph. Ingestion is written entirely with
\texttt{MERGE} rather than \texttt{CREATE}, and stamps every node it
touches with \texttt{firstSeenAt} on creation and \texttt{lastUpdatedAt}
on every write, which gives a per-node provenance trail without a
separate audit table.

An instructive dead end is worth recording. Nodes created before that
provenance stamping existed have neither property, and a script
(\texttt{seal\_legacy\_batch.py}) was written to tag them as a legacy
batch so they could be identified later. By the time it ran, every node
already carried some other date field, so it had nothing to seal and the
\texttt{legacyBatch} property never marked a single real node. The
mechanism that actually solved the underlying problem — separating
material worth keeping from accumulated noise — was not a timestamp at
all but the curation signal (\S\ref{sec:methodology-curation}). The
lesson generalises: provenance metadata answers "when did this arrive",
which turned out not to be the question that needed answering.

\section{Network Analysis Engine}

\texttt{3\_analyze\_network.py} is the component behind the closed line
of work described in \S\ref{sec:network-methods}: it exports nodes and
edges to CSV, builds two network layers in igraph, computes exact
PageRank, exact betweenness and Leiden community detection, and writes
results back with batched \texttt{UNWIND} statements, entirely offline
from the exported CSVs with no live database connection required. It is
documented here, briefly, only because two of its outputs — PageRank and
betweenness per account — are still consumed downstream by the export
script (\S\ref{sec:website}) and therefore still reach the live site
(\S\ref{sec:ranking}); it is not run on any regular cadence, and the
account coverage it provides reflects whichever pass last touched a given
account rather than the current graph.

\section{Event Enrichment Services}

The event phase is where the system depends on components it does not
control: two sentence-transformer models, an NLI model, and a hosted
language model. Robustness therefore had to be built at the boundary. The
extractor selects between a local model server and two cloud providers,
marks a provider as exhausted for the remainder of a run once it has
failed, and switches to the alternative rather than retrying
indefinitely; a cap on rate-limit waiting prevents a provider's
\texttt{Retry-After} header from stalling a run for minutes at a time
(DD-033). The mechanism was validated by two distinct failure modes in
practice — quota exhaustion and genuine network errors — and handled both
by switching providers within ninety seconds.

\section{The Discovery Website}
\label{sec:website}

The public artifact is a static site: \texttt{5\_export\_dashboard\_data.py}
reads the graph and writes a single JSON file, and
\texttt{site/} contains hand-written HTML, CSS and vanilla JavaScript
that consumes it, deployed to Cloudflare Workers as a static-assets
bundle (\texttt{site/wrangler.jsonc}, Workers Assets). There is no
backend, no framework and no build step, which is a deliberate
consequence of the dataset's size — the publishable events fit
comfortably in a file of a few hundred kilobytes — and of the deployment
constraint that the site must be reconnectable from a plain repository
without configuration (DD-043, DD-044). The export is read-only and can
be re-run after any extraction or resolution pass to refresh the data
file, but regenerating that file and publishing it are two separate
manual steps: neither a \texttt{git push} nor running the export script
triggers \texttt{wrangler deploy}, a gap that went unnoticed for some
time because the site had already been deployed once with older data and
so never looked broken, only stale (DD-053).

Six design decisions are worth reporting as part of the thesis's applied
contribution.

\textbf{The category menu is fully dynamic.} There is no editorial list
of families hard-coded anywhere. The "what" axis is assembled on each
load from the union of the event categories and per-event artistic tags
actually present in the data, with real counts, ordered by volume. The
menu therefore reflects what the upstream human curation has prioritised
rather than an assertion in the code, and it changes shape as the
curation does.

\textbf{Recommendation without accounts.} Session context is stored in
\texttt{localStorage}: category weights increment when a visitor filters
by or opens an event in that category, decay daily, and are dropped below
a floor; recently seen events are down-weighted so the home page rotates.
This yields the $C$ component of the ranking formula with no login, no
server and no personal data.

\textbf{Similarity without exposing embeddings.} The export computes
cosine similarity across all events and stores, per event, the
identifiers of its five nearest neighbours. The 384-dimensional vectors
themselves are discarded after use and never reach the client, which
keeps the payload small and avoids publishing a representation of the
corpus that was never intended to be public.

\textbf{Network analysis stays under the hood.} PageRank, betweenness and
community structure influence the ordering of events and occasionally
surface as a badge, but the first version deliberately has no dedicated
organiser-profile or methodology pages. This was a scoping decision:
those screens are more legible as thesis material than as product, and
the remaining screens (Explore, Map, organiser profiles) are listed as
future work in \S\ref{sec:future-work}.

\textbf{Publication is gated by human review, not by the extraction
pipeline alone.} Every \texttt{:Event} node is created with a second
label, \texttt{:PendingReview}, and both the JSON export and the Excel
export exclude it by default, so a newly detected event stays invisible
on the live site until it has been triaged. Triage happens in
\texttt{review\_events.py}, a small local Streamlit application with
three actions per event — approve (removes the label), edit (an inline
form that rewrites fields directly on the node), and reject — where
reject is deliberately soft, adding a \texttt{:Rejected} label rather
than deleting the node, so a mistaken rejection is always recoverable
(DD-047). This reuses the same label-based status pattern already used
for the automated Layer-3 gate (\S\ref{sec:event-pipeline}) rather than
introducing a second status system for human decisions. In practice, the
first real batch of pending events showed that most out-of-scope items
shared one property — a \texttt{geoZone} outside France, inherited from
the publishing account at creation time — and reviewing those one by one
was disproportionate to the judgement each one required, so the
interface was extended with a filter on that property and a bulk-reject
action scoped to whatever the filter currently shows (DD-052). The
trade-off is explicit and unresolved: \texttt{geoZone} describes the
account, not the event, so the bulk action is a heuristic for the
common case rather than a per-event guarantee, and it remains at the
reviewer's discretion to apply.

\textbf{Event content is bilingual only going forward, by design.} The
interface text — menus, filters, labels — already switched between
Spanish and French; the content of individual events did not, because
\texttt{title} and \texttt{description} were written once, in Spanish
only, by Layer 3 (\S\ref{sec:event-pipeline}). Rather than translate the
existing corpus after the fact, French versions are now generated by the
same LLM call that extracts the event (DD-051), and the frontend falls
back to the Spanish text whenever the French field is absent rather than
leaving a blank card. The scope was deliberately narrowed to new events
only, so the two language modes are not at parity and will not be until
either enough of the corpus is re-extracted or a backfill pass is run;
translation quality has also not yet been separately validated.

\section{Thematic and Event Landscape}
\label{sec:thematic}

At RUN-021 the graph held 664 \texttt{:Event} nodes, of which 559 met the
validity criteria for publication and 170 passed the additional
requirement of resolved coordinates and entered the 2026-08-14 dashboard
export alongside 200 accounts.

The distribution of the exported events by category is markedly uneven:
gastronomy 51, training and workshops 27, institutional 22, visual arts
19, music 15, performing arts 9, community 9, festivals 6, audiovisual 6,
politics 3 and academic 3. Gastronomy is thus the single largest
category, at 30\% of the exported set and 35\% of the full graph.

This is a finding about the pipeline, not about the cultural field. The
manual curation actively avoids gastronomy in favour of the project's
declared priorities; the curator marks such accounts explicitly as "not
applicable" rather than assigning them an artistic type. Gastronomy
dominates because gastronomic posts are easy to scrape and easy to detect
as events — they announce dates, venues and prices in exactly the
register the detection layers reward — not because the project set out to
collect them. The response was to demote the category in the interface
rather than to filter it out of the data, keeping it available as a
filter while removing it from the default prominence it would otherwise
take purely on volume.

falls into an adjacent category or none. Since literature is one of the

interface detail, and it motivated the addition of per-event artistic
tags (DD-042) — which, being prospective only, do not yet cover the
events already in the graph.

\S\ref{sec:objectives} — have grown to 57 events, a large enough share
(7.6\%) to be worth tracking as its own line rather than folding it
silently into the count.

\section{Geographic Distribution}
\label{sec:geographic}

Of the 559 events meeting the validity criteria on 2026-08-13, 528
(94.5\%) had an associated \texttt{:Location} node and 478 (85.5\%) had
real coordinates. The two shortfalls have different causes: 31 events
carry no location text at all, because the caption never stated one, and
50 have location text that either failed to resolve in Nominatim or had
not yet been through a geocoding pass. Only the first group is
irrecoverable without a change to the extraction prompt.

Against the larger, current catalogue of 751 events, the shortfall is
wider rather than narrower: 91.7\% carry some location text (a venue name
or address, short of a full coordinate pair), but only 47.4\% have
resolved coordinates. The gap between those two figures — text present
but unresolved — is the geocoding backlog rather than a caption-level
absence, consistent with an intermittent Nominatim failure observed on a
subsequent full run (zero coordinates resolved out of 276 attempted,
cause unconfirmed) rather than with a change in how often captions state
a location at all. Because the discovery site excludes ungeocoded events
from anything geography-dependent rather than approximating their
position (\S\ref{sec:geo}), this backlog directly determines how much of
the catalogue is usable in a geography-first product, and geocoding
coverage is consequently one of the more consequential, and cheaper to
fix, current data-quality gaps.

\section{Data Quality Findings}
\label{sec:data-quality}

Data quality is reported here as a result rather than deferred to a
limitations footnote, because the error analysis is one of the more
transferable outputs of the project.

The evaluation trajectory described in \S\ref{sec:eval-design} is the
headline: agreement between the pipeline and blind human classification
moved from 58\% (100 posts, 2026-08-09) through 82.4\% precision and
85.8\% recall (495 posts, 2026-08-12, F1 = 0.841) to 96.7\% agreement with
97.3\% precision and approximately 90\% recall (150 posts, RUN-020,
F1 $\approx$ 0.935). The largest single gain came from one conditional
statement — making node creation depend on the language model's own
invitation and upcoming flags, which it had been computing and ignoring —
and it alone accounts for most of the roughly nine-point F1 improvement
between the two rounds; the remaining, smaller gain came from the
reference-set and prompt changes made alongside it (\S\ref{sec:eval-design}).

A manual review of a targeted sample sharpens the picture where the
aggregate figures are most flattering. The twenty institutional events
with the highest resonance scores were classified by hand: roughly eight
were genuine public events correctly categorised; roughly seven were not
events at all — interviews, podcasts, press releases, appointment
announcements, a private meeting — and had passed the public-invitation
gate that should have stopped them; and roughly five were real events
placed in the wrong category, including two visual-art installations, a
concert tour, a restaurant menu in Spain unrelated to the project, and a
language-course enrolment that an explicit prompt instruction was
supposed to exclude. Twelve of twenty problematic is a much worse rate
than the aggregate figures suggest, and the discrepancy is itself the
finding: the sample was selected by highest resonance within the
institutional category, which is precisely where the pipeline's two known
weaknesses concentrate — a leaky invitation gate and a tendency to type
an event by the nature of the account that published it rather than by
the content of the post. Aggregate accuracy over a random sample and
accuracy within a targeted high-visibility slice are different quantities,
and a discovery product surfaces the second.

Deduplication performance is measurable on the same run. Over 664 events,
the resolver evaluated 220{,}116 pairs in roughly twenty seconds,
identified 68 duplicate pairs, and consolidated them through 189
relationship redirections, transitive chains included. Of the pairs it
rejected, 12{,}375 failed on similarity, 165{,}018 on the date window,
and 6 on the geographic-conflict guardrail — the last group being
precisely the failure cases that motivated the guardrail
(\S\ref{sec:event-resolver}).

\section{From a Diaspora Network to a Cultural-Relevance Network}
\label{sec:scope-evolution}

The project's scope changed twice. It began as a network of Colombian
diaspora accounts, seeded from a single institutional account, the
Colombian consulate in Paris. As the network grew through automatic
relationship discovery (\texttt{RELATED\_TO}), it broadened to Latin
American diaspora accounts more generally. Both of these were still
nationality-based criteria: an account or event qualified because of
\emph{who} was behind it.

The second, more consequential change replaced that criterion with a
purely cultural one: an account or event qualifies if it is culturally
relevant, with explicit priority given to photography, cinema,
literature, theatre, and locally-organized community events, regardless
of the nationality or cultural origin of the organizers behind it. This
is a stricter methodological change than it might first appear, because
it also required removing a bonus that had previously been present in the
discovery website's relevance-ranking formula (\S\ref{sec:ranking}) — a
multiplicative $\times 1.05$ applied to any event carrying a non-null
\texttt{culturalIdentity} value, a field which in this corpus is
overwhelmingly populated with Colombian and Latin American values.
Retaining that bonus after the scope change would have produced a ranking
signal that was formally neutral (it ranks \emph{cultural} events) while
materially still favouring one set of national identities over every
other one present in the same graph; removing it was necessary for the
ranking formula to be honest about what it actually measures. The field
itself was kept, as an optional filter a visitor can choose to apply —
the distinction being between an identity that a user selects and one
that the system applies on their behalf without telling them.

The same audit produced a negative result worth recording: community
detection was confirmed to be purely topological, computed only from
graph edges, so \texttt{culturalIdentity} never entered the community
structure through a back door. The bias in the ranking formula was
explicit and removable; the more interesting bias was not.

That one is the reference set of the event detector's first layer. Its
roughly 100 phrases had been written entirely in the vocabulary of
Colombian diaspora events, and the blind evaluation of 100 posts
(\S\ref{sec:eval-design}) measured the consequence: general and French
accounts lost real events at 12.9\%, against 4.3\% for Colombian and
Latin American accounts — nearly three times the rate, on events with
explicit dates and venues that were unambiguous to a human reader. A
project that had already decided, at the level of stated policy, to
include cultural activity regardless of origin was still, at the level of
implementation, seeing that activity less well. This is the strongest
argument in the thesis for treating the scope change as methodological
rather than administrative: the nationality criterion was not only a
boundary drawn around the corpus, it was embedded in the instrument that
produced the corpus, where it was invisible until measured.

What this change gained, beyond ranking-formula honesty, is a criterion
that is easier to defend and to operationalize consistently:
\emph{is this cultural, and does it fall within one of these priority
domains} is a judgment that can be applied uniformly to any account the
discovery process surfaces. \emph{Is this part of the Latin American
diaspora}, by contrast, required an ad hoc call for every borderline
case — a French venue that regularly hosts Colombian artists, a Latin
American cultural institute whose visitors are mostly French — that the
pipeline had no principled way to resolve consistently. What it cost is
some of the project's original sociological specificity: the initial
research question was, in part, about how \emph{one} diaspora organizes
culturally in a host country, and that question is necessarily diluted
once the object of study is cultural activity in general. This thesis
handles that trade-off by keeping the diaspora-specific reading as one
lens applied to a subset of the network (wherever \texttt{culturalIdentity}
data supports it), rather than as the network's defining boundary.

\section{On Data Quality and the Limits of Automation}

The project's most consistent lesson is that automated signals fail
silently and confidently. This is not a general complaint about
automation but a specific, repeated pattern in these results, visible in
four independent places. The account classifier assigned high scores to
accounts in the wrong country, because the geographic signal it trusted
did not contain the field it assumed (DD-031). The geocoder, offered a
fallback, returned coordinates that were precise and wrong rather than
absent (\S\ref{sec:geo}). The date extractor filled in a day of the month
that no caption ever stated (DD-034), and later accepted a theatre
season as a date (DD-037). The language model typed events with a label
meaning "not a cultural event" while the surrounding code created them
anyway (DD-039). In none of these cases did the system report low
confidence; in every case the failure was found by a human reading the
output.

The corresponding lesson about human curation is more qualified than
"humans are better". Human curation was the more reliable trust signal
here, and replacing automated account scoring with it was the right
decision on the evidence — under 10\% of automatically selected accounts
survived manual review. But it does not scale, it embeds a single
researcher's judgement, and the evaluation protocol that validated the
event pipeline measured agreement with that same judgement rather than
with any external ground truth (\S\ref{sec:eval-design}). What the
project actually demonstrates is narrower and more useful: automation was
most reliable where it was constrained to deterministic, checkable
operations (does this caption contain a date; do these two coordinates
fall inside this bounding box; do these two country names conflict) and
least reliable where it was asked to make the judgement of relevance
itself. The design that emerged puts deterministic gates in front of and
behind the probabilistic components, and reserves the relevance judgement
for a person.

\todo{Extend with the implication for comparable projects, stated as
design guidance rather than as a moral: put a cheap deterministic gate in
front of any expensive probabilistic component, prefer absence to
plausible-looking approximation in any user-facing field, and instrument
production runs at least as well as rehearsals (DD-038).}

\section{Practical Implications: The Website as Intervention}

The discovery site is not only a dissemination channel but, in a modest
sense, a sociological intervention: it makes a set of events visible
together that were previously legible only to whoever already followed
the right accounts, and its ranking formula makes an explicit editorial
claim about what counts as relevant rather than leaving that claim
implicit in an opaque feed algorithm. Two design decisions from the
implementation are worth reading this way rather than only as engineering
choices. The category menu is assembled dynamically from whatever themes
and tags are actually present in the curated data, with no editorial list
hard-coded anywhere (\S\ref{sec:website}); the taxonomy the visitor sees
is therefore a direct, live reflection of what the curation process has
prioritised, not a claim fixed in advance and left to drift out of sync
with it. And the removal of the nationality-linked ranking bonus
(\S\ref{sec:scope-evolution}) is a worked example of an ordinary product
decision — a multiplier in a formula — that was also, on inspection, a
political one: a signal that looked neutral on its face was quietly
favouring one set of identities over every other one present in the same
graph, and fixing that required treating a scoring weight as a claim
about fairness, not only about relevance. Whether the artifact met the
project's fourth, deliberately human objective — leading to actually
meeting someone through it (\S\ref{sec:objectives}) — is addressed on its
own terms in \S\ref{sec:closing-reflection}, since it is not a question a
usage metric can answer.

\section{Methodological Reflections}

Four things would be done differently starting over, each supported by a
specific failure already documented in this thesis rather than by
hindsight in general. First, the human-curation trust signal
(\S\ref{sec:methodology-curation}) would be built in from the start
instead of arrived at after an automated classifier had already run long
enough to accumulate a network's worth of data around a criterion later
judged wrong under 90\% of the time. Second, the semantic reference set
behind event detection would be written to the scope the project intends
to end up with, not the scope it starts from — \S\ref{sec:scope-evolution}
shows that an inclusion criterion believed to be conceptual was in fact
encoded, measurably, in a list of a hundred phrases, where it went
unnoticed until a blind evaluation surfaced it. Third, production runs
would be instrumented for audit from the first run, not after one proved
unauditable (DD-038): the discipline described in
\S\ref{sec:research-ops} was a response to a specific incident, not a
plan followed from day one. Fourth, every field a person will eventually
read would default to explicit absence rather than a plausible-looking
fallback — the geocoding, date-extraction and classification failures of
\S\ref{sec:data-quality} share this one shape, a system reporting
something rather than nothing even when it does not know, and each was
found only by a human noticing the output was wrong, never by the system
flagging its own uncertainty.

\section{Summary of Contributions}

The methodological contribution is the pivot from automated to human
curation as the account-level trust signal (\S\ref{sec:methodology-curation}),
evidenced rather than asserted — under 10\% of automatically-selected
accounts survived manual review — together with the blind
human-versus-pipeline evaluation protocol that drove three successive
redesigns of the event-detection logic and moved measured agreement from
58\% to 96.7\% and precision from 56\% to 97.3\%
(\S\ref{sec:eval-design}), and the research-operations discipline —
real-time decision log, run log, dry-run-first design — that made every
number in this thesis traceable to the run that produced it
(\S\ref{sec:research-ops}). The empirical contribution is narrower than
originally planned and is stated as such: a systematically detected and
increasingly accurate catalogue of cultural events (751 at the time of
writing, \S\ref{sec:thematic}), a transparent account of where the
pipeline is and is not reliable (\S\ref{sec:data-quality}), and one
closed, superseded structural finding about the account network kept for
the record rather than advanced as a current result
(\S\ref{sec:future-work-network}). The applied contribution is the
deployed bilingual discovery website itself (\S\ref{sec:website}), with
its full publication lifecycle — detection, staged human review, ranking,
retention and manual deploy (\S\ref{sec:lifecycle}) — documented as part
of the method rather than left as background infrastructure.

\section{Answering the Research Question}

The thesis's central question asked whether culturally-relevant activity
in the Paris region could be systematically mapped, analysed and made
discoverable from public Instagram data. The practical sub-question has
the clearest answer: yes, to the extent that a live, bilingual, static
website now surfaces a continuously refreshed, human-reviewed catalogue
of such events, with its own retention policy and no dependency on a
backend or user accounts (\S\ref{sec:website}, \S\ref{sec:lifecycle}).
The thematic sub-question has a qualified answer: the pipeline classifies
detected events into the project's priority domains with measured,
improving accuracy (\S\ref{sec:eval-design}), but two of the four
founding thematic pillars — photography and cinema — remain
under-detected relative to their curated account base, a gap traced to
the fixed sixteen-label taxonomy rather than to an absence of activity
(\S\ref{sec:thematic}). The structural sub-question — who are the
connective actors in this network — has the least settled answer of the
three: it was addressed once, early, before the scope broadened and
before curation-based cleanup ran, and that answer is reported as a
closed, dated finding rather than revalidated here
(\S\ref{sec:future-work-network}); answering it properly on the current,
curated graph is left as future work rather than claimed prematurely.

\section{Limitations}

The limitations catalogued in \S\ref{sec:limitations} amount to five
boundaries on how far these results generalise. The corpus is
single-platform: organisations that announce primarily elsewhere are
structurally invisible to it. It is geographically concentrated but not
geographically clean: Île-de-France is the intended center of gravity,
but institutions physically outside France enter through curation and
through automatic discovery alike. The evaluation measures agreement with
a single annotator's judgement, not a consensus ground truth. The
corpus's boundary is set by how much one researcher can review by hand,
which is the direct cost of replacing automated scoring with human
curation (\S\ref{sec:methodology-curation}) — a trade this thesis argues
was the right one on the evidence, but a real one. And every figure in
this thesis is a snapshot of a graph and a pipeline that were both still
changing as it was written, stated with its run of origin for exactly
that reason.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future-work}

\subsection{Revisiting Network Analysis on the Curated Graph}
\label{sec:future-work-network}

The account-network analysis described in \S\ref{sec:network-methods} —
exact PageRank and betweenness centrality, Leiden community detection at
three resolutions, k-core decomposition, participation coefficient and
E-I index, computed separately over a social layer (mentions, tags,
co-authorship) and an algorithmic layer (Instagram's own
\texttt{relatedProfiles} suggestions) — was run once, at RUN-015
(2026-07-24), against 7{,}269 \texttt{:Account} nodes, before the scope
broadened beyond the Latin American diaspora and before
\texttt{cleanup\_legacy\_accounts.py} removed the uncurated periphery.
At that snapshot the graph was 92.5\% individual accounts against a small
institutional core (cultural institutions 2.5\%, state institutions
2.2\%, commercial accounts 2.1\%, media 0.7\%); both layers were
dominated by a single giant component (85--90\% of nodes); Leiden
modularity exceeded 0.85 at every resolution tested; and, most
consequentially, some of the accounts with the highest betweenness and
PageRank on both layers had almost no posts of their own, their
centrality derived entirely from being mentioned and tagged by others —
the single most direct empirical argument, in this project's own record,
for why an account-selection criterion should not read relevance off an
account's own content.

That analysis was never repeated, and this thesis treats it as a closed,
dated finding rather than a current result, for the reasons given in
\S\ref{sec:network-methods}: the criterion it was built to serve was
retired, and every precondition of the snapshot above — the corpus's
scope, its composition, and which accounts count as noise — has since
changed. What remains genuinely open, and is left as future work rather
than attempted here, is whether the same structural pattern (a small
institutional bridging core amid a large organic periphery) survives on
the graph as it stands today, restricted to the subgraph that human
curation has actually validated rather than the full discovered
periphery. That question is now cheaper to ask than it was at RUN-015 —
the curated subgraph is a small fraction of the 7,269-node graph the
first analysis ran over — and, unlike the original account-selection use
case, would not be asking the network to do a job human curation already
does better; it would be asking a documented, sociologically-motivated
question about a graph the project already has.

\subsection{Pipeline and Product}

Beyond the network-analysis question above, the project's own planning
notes and open items point to a concrete list: a new account classifier
with explicit geography and cultural-relevance dimensions, should account
discovery ever need to scale beyond what manual curation can review; an
expanded seed set from Latin American consulates in Paris (DD-022 lists
the handles, several still unverified); a revised harvest cycle
prioritising cheap profile scraping before expensive post scraping; a
literature label added to the event taxonomy and a backfill decision for
\texttt{eventArtTags} (DD-042), directly motivated by the
under-detection of photography and cinema events noted in
\S\ref{sec:thematic}; resolution of the open question in DD-035 on
ongoing exhibitions; normalisation of stylised Unicode digits in captions
if confirmed frequent (DD-036); the remaining site screens — a
clickable arrondissement and commune map, an explore view, and
organiser-profile pages; a backfill decision for
\texttt{titleFr}/\texttt{descriptionFr} on events created before DD-051,
together with a first quality check of the translations it produces;
whether DeepSeek should remain in the default cloud cascade at all, given
the empty-response failures of DD-050; and collapsing data export, static
page generation and deploy (\S\ref{sec:lifecycle}) into a single command
so publishing stops being the step that gets forgotten (DD-053).
